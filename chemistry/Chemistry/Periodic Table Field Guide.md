# The Periodic Table Field Guide: Where All 118 Elements Occur

> [!abstract] What this note covers
> A field guide to the natural occurrence, geographical distribution, extraction or production, and practical uses of every chemical element. It is designed to complement [[Chemistry/Course/07 - The Periodic Table and Valence Electrons|the periodic-table lesson]] by connecting atomic identity to real places, rocks, water, air, living things, and technology.

## How to read each entry

- **Where:** named regions or natural environments where the element is known to occur. A region is an example, not a claim that every location contains an economically mineable deposit.
- **Natural form:** whether it occurs as a native element, dissolved species, gas, mineral constituent, or trace impurity.
- **Obtaining it and uses:** the general industrial route and representative applications.
- **Natural occurrence is not the same as a mineable resource.** An element can be widespread but highly dilute. Many metals are chemically bound in minerals, not present as shiny, pure metal.
- **Important limit:** elements 95–118 and several others have no meaningful commercial natural deposits. Their entries describe laboratory production instead of inventing a mine or island.

## Big picture: where the elements come from

1. **The early universe** produced mostly hydrogen and helium, plus tiny amounts of lithium.
2. **Stars** made many elements through nuclear fusion and other stellar reactions.
3. **Explosive and neutron-rich astrophysical events** made many heavier nuclei, including much of the gold and other heavy elements.
4. **Radioactive decay** creates daughter elements in rocks, including helium, radon, and tiny natural traces of several radioactive elements.
5. **Earth's geology** concentrates some elements into mineral deposits through crystallization, hot fluids, weathering, sedimentation, evaporation, and other processes.
6. **Human nuclear reactions** produce many short-lived elements that are not available as natural deposits.

## Part I — Elements 1–20

### 1. Hydrogen (H)
- **Where in nature:** Water in oceans, rivers, lakes, groundwater, ice and soil moisture; organic molecules in trees, crops, animals and microorganisms; methane and other hydrocarbons in natural-gas reservoirs. Stars contain overwhelmingly hydrogen-rich matter.
- **Natural form:** On Earth, mostly chemically bonded in water and organic compounds; only small amounts of free hydrogen gas occur naturally.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Industry produces hydrogen mainly from natural gas or by electrolysis of water. It is used to make ammonia fertilizer, refine fuels, manufacture chemicals and supply fuel cells.

### 2. Helium (He)
- **Where in nature:** Trace amounts in air; enriched underground in some natural-gas reservoirs, including deposits in the United States, Qatar and Algeria. It is generated when uranium and thorium decay in rocks, then can migrate and become trapped in gas reservoirs.
- **Natural form:** A noble gas, often mixed with natural gas; helium escapes Earth's atmosphere easily because it is very light.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from helium-rich gas streams and purified. Used for MRI and scientific magnet cooling, leak detection, specialized gas systems and some balloons.

### 3. Lithium (Li)
- **Where in nature:** Spodumene-bearing pegmatites at Greenbushes in Western Australia and other hard-rock deposits; lithium-rich brines beneath the Salar de Atacama in Chile and salt flats in Argentina and Bolivia.
- **Natural form:** Mostly lithium ions in minerals or salty groundwater, not native lithium metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from mineral concentrates or brines, then refined into lithium chemicals. Used in rechargeable batteries, specialty glass, ceramics and some greases.

### 4. Beryllium (Be)
- **Where in nature:** Beryl-bearing granitic pegmatites in Brazil, Madagascar, the United States and elsewhere; emerald is a green variety of beryl. Other beryllium minerals occur in specialized igneous rocks.
- **Natural form:** Bound in silicate minerals; not found as native metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from selected beryllium ores using controlled chemical processing. Used in lightweight, stiff aerospace alloys, X-ray equipment and specialized instruments. Beryllium dust is highly hazardous.

### 5. Boron (B)
- **Where in nature:** Borate deposits in arid basins, including California's Mojave Desert and western Türkiye; boron compounds also occur in some saline lakes, evaporite deposits and soils.
- **Natural form:** Borate minerals and dissolved borate species, rather than free elemental boron.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Borate minerals are mined or recovered from brines. Boron compounds are used in borosilicate glass, fiberglass, detergents, fertilizers and semiconductor materials.

### 6. Carbon (C)
- **Where in nature:** Wood, bark, leaves, roots and all known living organisms; atmospheric carbon dioxide; dissolved carbon in oceans; coal seams; graphite deposits; diamond-bearing rocks; limestone and other carbonate rocks.
- **Natural form:** Elemental graphite and diamond, plus carbon in enormous numbers of compounds. Plants capture atmospheric CO₂ during photosynthesis and build it into biomass.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Sourced from graphite, carbon-rich feedstocks and chemical processing. Used in steelmaking, electrodes, activated carbon, carbon fiber, plastics, fuels and countless organic chemicals.

### 7. Nitrogen (N)
- **Where in nature:** About 78% of dry air; also in soil organic matter, decomposing leaves, animal waste, proteins, DNA, and dissolved nitrate or ammonium in water and soils.
- **Natural form:** Mainly N₂ gas in the atmosphere; living things and soils contain nitrogen compounds.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated from air by industrial gas-separation processes. Used to manufacture ammonia and fertilizers, preserve food, create inert atmospheres and provide cryogenic cooling.

### 8. Oxygen (O)
- **Where in nature:** About 21% of dry air; water in rivers, lakes, oceans and ice; quartz, feldspar, clay, limestone, iron oxides and almost all common rocks.
- **Natural form:** O₂ gas in the atmosphere and dissolved in water, but most Earth's oxygen atoms are chemically bound in water and minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced commercially by air separation or water electrolysis. Used in steelmaking, medicine, water treatment, welding and chemical production.

### 9. Fluorine (F)
- **Where in nature:** Fluorite deposits in China, Mexico, South Africa and other regions; also occurs in apatite, some micas and fluoride-bearing groundwater.
- **Natural form:** Fluoride ions in minerals and water. Free fluorine gas is so reactive that it is not found accumulating naturally on Earth.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Fluorite is processed into fluoride chemicals; elemental fluorine requires specialized industrial electrolysis. Fluorine chemistry is used in fluoropolymers, refrigerants, pharmaceuticals and other specialty chemicals. Fluoride compounds are used in dental care.

### 10. Neon (Ne)
- **Where in nature:** Extremely dilute in Earth's atmosphere, mixed with nitrogen, oxygen and argon. It has no ordinary concentrated mineral deposit.
- **Natural form:** Monatomic noble gas.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered as a by-product of industrial air separation. Used in neon discharge lighting, indicators, specialized lasers and research equipment.

### 11. Sodium (Na)
- **Where in nature:** Halite (rock salt) beds, salt domes, evaporated ancient seas, salt lakes and seawater; also in many clay minerals and feldspars.
- **Natural form:** Almost entirely sodium ions in salts and minerals because sodium metal reacts readily with water and air.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Sodium chloride is mined or recovered from brines. Sodium compounds are used in glass, soaps, detergents, paper, food processing and chemical manufacturing. Sodium metal is produced by specialized electrolysis for limited industrial uses.

### 12. Magnesium (Mg)
- **Where in nature:** Magnesite and dolomite rocks; seawater and salt-lake brines; minerals in many igneous rocks; chlorophyll in green leaves.
- **Natural form:** Magnesium ions in minerals and water, and bound in biological molecules.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced from magnesium-rich brines or mineral ores. Used in lightweight alloys, refractory materials, fireworks, and compounds for agriculture and medicine.

### 13. Aluminium (Al)
- **Where in nature:** Bauxite deposits in Guinea, Australia and Brazil; clay-rich soils; feldspar and other aluminosilicate minerals in granite and many common rocks. Egypt also has silica and aluminosilicate-rich geological materials, though the presence of aluminium in rock does not by itself mean it is bauxite ore.
- **Natural form:** Strongly bound to oxygen and other elements; native aluminium metal is not a normal surface occurrence.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Bauxite is refined to alumina, then electrolyzed to aluminium metal. Used in cans, aircraft, electrical cables, vehicles, windows and building materials.

### 14. Silicon (Si)
- **Where in nature:** Quartz veins, beach and desert sands, sandstone, granite, feldspar, clay and most common crustal rocks. Egypt has silica-rich sand deposits in parts of Sinai and around the Gulf of Suez.
- **Natural form:** Mostly silicon dioxide (silica) and silicate minerals, not free silicon metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Silica is processed into glass or reduced to silicon; semiconductor-grade silicon needs additional purification. Used in computer chips, solar cells, glass, concrete and silicone materials.

### 15. Phosphorus (P)
- **Where in nature:** Phosphate rock in Morocco and Western Sahara, Florida, China and other regions; phosphate-bearing deposits in Egypt including Abu Tartur and areas near the Red Sea. Also in bones, teeth, guano, soil, DNA, ATP and cell membranes.
- **Natural form:** Phosphate minerals and phosphate ions; free elemental phosphorus is not normally found in surface environments because it reacts readily.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Phosphate rock is mined and processed into fertilizer and industrial chemicals. Phosphorus is essential to biological energy transfer and plant growth.

### 16. Sulfur (S)
- **Where in nature:** Volcanic areas, hydrothermal vents, hot springs, sulfur-bearing sedimentary rocks and some evaporite deposits. Sulfur is also recovered from oil and natural-gas processing because sulfur compounds occur in many fossil fuels.
- **Natural form:** Native sulfur near some volcanic systems, plus sulfide and sulfate minerals and dissolved sulfur compounds.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Collected from natural deposits in limited cases or recovered during fuel processing. Used to make sulfuric acid, fertilizers, rubber chemicals and many industrial compounds.

### 17. Chlorine (Cl)
- **Where in nature:** Seawater, salt lakes, underground brines, halite deposits and chloride-rich groundwater. The Dead Sea and other evaporative basins concentrate salts.
- **Natural form:** Mainly chloride ions in salts and water; free chlorine gas is not a stable widespread natural reservoir.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Commonly produced by electrolysis of concentrated brine. Used to disinfect water and manufacture PVC, bleach compounds and many chemicals. Chlorine gas is hazardous and requires industrial controls.

### 18. Argon (Ar)
- **Where in nature:** About 0.93% of dry air. Argon-40 also forms from radioactive decay of potassium-40 in rocks and can accumulate in some geological fluids.
- **Natural form:** Monatomic noble gas.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated from air in industrial plants. Used as a shielding gas for welding, in lamps, and in laboratories and manufacturing processes that need an inert atmosphere.

### 19. Potassium (K)
- **Where in nature:** Potash deposits in Saskatchewan, Canada, and sedimentary basins in Germany and Russia; feldspar and mica in granite; soil, groundwater and plant tissues.
- **Natural form:** Potassium ions in minerals, salts and living cells; pure potassium metal does not persist in wet natural environments.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Potash minerals are mined and processed into potassium salts. The main use is fertilizer, with additional uses in glass and chemical manufacturing.

### 20. Calcium (Ca)
- **Where in nature:** Limestone, chalk, marble, gypsum, shells, coral skeletons, bones and teeth. Egypt has extensive limestone formations along the Nile Valley and in desert regions.
- **Natural form:** Calcium carbonate, calcium sulfate, apatite and other minerals; calcium ions also occur in natural waters.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Limestone and gypsum are quarried; calcium compounds are made through mineral processing. Used in cement, lime, plaster, construction, agriculture and biological processes.

## Part II — Elements 21–40

### 21. Scandium (Sc)
- **Where in nature:** Scattered at low concentrations in many rocks and minerals; richer occurrences include thortveitite in Norway and Madagascar, and some rare-earth or uranium-processing residues.
- **Natural form:** Trace element in minerals, not native metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from selected ores or industrial by-products. Used in aluminium-scandium alloys and specialized lighting. Its scarcity in concentrated deposits makes production limited.

### 22. Titanium (Ti)
- **Where in nature:** Rutile and ilmenite in heavy-mineral beach sands and ancient shoreline deposits in Australia, South Africa, India and other regions; also in igneous rocks.
- **Natural form:** Titanium oxides and minerals, not native titanium metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Minerals are processed into titanium dioxide pigment or titanium metal. Used in paints, paper, some sunscreens, aircraft, implants and corrosion-resistant equipment.

### 23. Vanadium (V)
- **Where in nature:** Vanadium-bearing magnetite in South Africa, China, Russia and Brazil; also in some sedimentary deposits and petroleum residues.
- **Natural form:** Bound in iron oxides, other minerals and trace compounds.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from ores or industrial by-products. Used in strong steel alloys, catalysts and vanadium-flow batteries.

### 24. Chromium (Cr)
- **Where in nature:** Chromite-rich rocks in South Africa's Bushveld Complex, Kazakhstan, India, Türkiye and Zimbabwe.
- **Natural form:** Mainly chromite, an oxide mineral containing iron and chromium.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Chromite is processed into ferrochrome and other chromium materials. Used in stainless steel, corrosion-resistant alloys, plating and pigments. Some chromium compounds are toxic.

### 25. Manganese (Mn)
- **Where in nature:** Sedimentary manganese deposits in South Africa, Gabon, Australia and Brazil; manganese oxides in soils and seafloor nodules.
- **Natural form:** Oxide and carbonate minerals, and trace ions in soils and water.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Manganese ores are concentrated and processed. Used mainly in steelmaking, with additional uses in batteries, pigments and chemicals.

### 26. Iron (Fe)
- **Where in nature:** Hematite and magnetite in Western Australia's Pilbara, Brazil's Carajás region, South Africa and other iron-rich geological formations; iron also occurs in soils, meteorites and Earth's core. Egypt has iron-bearing deposits including the Bahariya Oasis region.
- **Natural form:** Mainly iron oxides and other minerals, not large masses of native iron at Earth's surface. Native iron is uncommon, though meteorites can contain iron-nickel metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Iron ore is concentrated and reduced in furnaces or other processes. Used to make steel for buildings, vehicles, tools, railways and machinery.

### 27. Cobalt (Co)
- **Where in nature:** Copper-cobalt deposits in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Zambia; nickel-bearing ores in Canada, Australia and other regions.
- **Natural form:** Usually a minor component of copper and nickel ores rather than a separate cobalt mineral deposit.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered mainly as a by-product of copper or nickel refining. Used in some rechargeable batteries, superalloys, catalysts and pigments.

### 28. Nickel (Ni)
- **Where in nature:** Nickel sulfide deposits around Sudbury in Canada and Norilsk in Russia; laterite deposits in Indonesia, the Philippines and New Caledonia.
- **Natural form:** Sulfide minerals and nickel-bearing weathered rocks; iron-nickel metal also occurs in meteorites.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from sulfide or laterite ores. Used in stainless steel, corrosion-resistant alloys, plating and some battery chemistries.

### 29. Copper (Cu)
- **Where in nature:** Porphyry copper deposits in Chile's Andes, Peru and Arizona; native copper in Michigan's Keweenaw Peninsula; copper minerals also occur in veins and oxidized zones. Copper-bearing mineralization is documented in parts of Egypt's Sinai and Eastern Desert.
- **Natural form:** Mostly sulfide or oxide minerals, with native copper in certain geological settings.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Ores are concentrated and smelted or leached, then copper is refined. Used in electrical wiring, motors, plumbing, electronics and heat exchangers.

### 30. Zinc (Zn)
- **Where in nature:** Sphalerite in sedimentary and hydrothermal deposits, including Australia's Mount Isa region, the Red Dog district in Alaska, and many deposits in China, Peru and elsewhere.
- **Natural form:** Mostly zinc sulfide and related minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Ore is concentrated and refined, often by roasting and electrolysis or other metallurgical processes. Used to galvanize steel, make brass, batteries and zinc compounds.

### 31. Gallium (Ga)
- **Where in nature:** Trace concentrations in bauxite and zinc ores, including deposits processed in China, Australia and other aluminium- and zinc-producing regions.
- **Natural form:** Dispersed in other minerals; not normally found as native gallium metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered as a by-product of aluminium or zinc processing. Gallium nitride and gallium arsenide are used in LEDs, radio-frequency electronics and specialized semiconductors.

### 32. Germanium (Ge)
- **Where in nature:** Trace amounts in certain zinc ores, coal and related industrial residues in China, Canada, Russia and other regions.
- **Natural form:** Dispersed in minerals rather than concentrated in common germanium ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from zinc processing or coal-related by-products. Used in fiber-optic systems, infrared optics, semiconductor devices and specialized solar technology.

### 33. Arsenic (As)
- **Where in nature:** Arsenopyrite and other arsenic-bearing minerals in hydrothermal ore deposits; examples occur in China, Morocco, parts of Europe and North America. Arsenic can also occur naturally in groundwater, sometimes at hazardous concentrations.
- **Natural form:** Sulfide minerals, arsenide minerals and dissolved arsenic species; native arsenic occurs but is not the main source.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Often recovered as a by-product of metal-ore processing. Some arsenic compounds have specialized semiconductor uses. Arsenic and many of its compounds are highly toxic.

### 34. Selenium (Se)
- **Where in nature:** Tiny amounts in sulfide ores, especially copper ores, and in some soils, sedimentary rocks and volcanic materials.
- **Natural form:** Often substitutes for sulfur in minerals or occurs in trace compounds.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered mainly from copper-refining residues. Used in glassmaking, electronic materials, some solar cells and specialized alloys.

### 35. Bromine (Br)
- **Where in nature:** Bromide-rich underground brines, including the Dead Sea region, subsurface brines in Arkansas in the United States, and some saline basins.
- **Natural form:** Bromide ions dissolved in brines; free bromine is not the common natural form.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from concentrated brines by industrial separation. Used to make specialized chemicals, some flame-retardant compounds, water-treatment chemicals and photographic materials.

### 36. Krypton (Kr)
- **Where in nature:** Very small quantities in Earth's atmosphere. No ordinary concentrated mineral deposit is known.
- **Natural form:** Monatomic noble gas.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during air separation. Used in specialized lighting, lasers, scientific equipment and certain insulated windows.

### 37. Rubidium (Rb)
- **Where in nature:** Trace amounts in potassium-rich minerals such as lepidolite and pollucite in granitic pegmatites in Canada, Namibia and other regions.
- **Natural form:** Rubidium ions substituting for potassium in minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from selected mineral-processing streams. Used in atomic clocks, research instruments and specialized electronics.

### 38. Strontium (Sr)
- **Where in nature:** Celestine and strontianite deposits in Spain, Mexico and China; also in seawater, carbonate rocks and some mineral springs.
- **Natural form:** Strontium sulfate or carbonate minerals and dissolved strontium ions.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Minerals are processed into strontium compounds. Used in ferrite magnets, ceramics and red-colored pyrotechnic formulations.

### 39. Yttrium (Y)
- **Where in nature:** Xenotime, monazite and other rare-earth minerals in China, Australia, Brazil and other rare-earth-producing regions.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with rare-earth elements in minerals, rather than found as native metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during rare-earth processing. Used in phosphors, lasers, high-performance ceramics and electronic materials.

### 40. Zirconium (Zr)
- **Where in nature:** Zircon crystals in beach sands and ancient heavy-mineral deposits in Australia, South Africa, India and other regions; also in certain igneous rocks.
- **Natural form:** Mainly zircon (zirconium silicate) and baddeleyite.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Zircon is processed into zirconium compounds or metal. Used in ceramics, corrosion-resistant equipment and nuclear-reactor components.

## Part III — Elements 41–60

### 41. Niobium (Nb)
- **Where in nature:** Pyrochlore-rich carbonatite deposits, especially around Araxá in Brazil and Niobec in Canada; also in columbite minerals.
- **Natural form:** Oxide minerals containing niobium.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from niobium-bearing ores. Used in high-strength steel, superconducting magnets and specialized alloys.

### 42. Molybdenum (Mo)
- **Where in nature:** Molybdenite in deposits such as Climax and Henderson in Colorado, and copper-molybdenum deposits in Chile and Peru.
- **Natural form:** Mainly molybdenite, a molybdenum sulfide.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Mined directly or recovered as a copper-mining by-product. Used in heat-resistant steel, catalysts and industrial machinery.

### 43. Technetium (Tc)
- **Where in nature:** Only minute, short-lived traces arise from natural nuclear processes in uranium-bearing materials. There are no conventional technetium ore deposits.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes produced in tiny quantities.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced mainly in nuclear reactors. Technetium-99m is widely used in medical diagnostic imaging; other isotopes are used in research.

### 44. Ruthenium (Ru)
- **Where in nature:** Platinum-group metal ores in South Africa's Bushveld Complex and nickel-copper deposits in Russia and Canada.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with platinum-group minerals and other metal ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during platinum-group metal refining. Used in electronics, catalysts and specialized alloys.

### 45. Rhodium (Rh)
- **Where in nature:** Platinum-group metal deposits, especially South Africa's Bushveld region and Russia's Norilsk district.
- **Natural form:** Rare metal mixed with platinum-group ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered as a by-product of platinum and nickel mining. Used in vehicle exhaust catalysts, chemical catalysts and reflective coatings.

### 46. Palladium (Pd)
- **Where in nature:** Nickel-copper sulfide ores in Russia and Canada, and platinum-group deposits in South Africa.
- **Natural form:** Associated with platinum-group minerals and sulfide ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during metal refining. Used in catalytic converters, electronics, hydrogen-related technology and chemical catalysts.

### 47. Silver (Ag)
- **Where in nature:** Native silver and silver minerals in Mexico, Peru, Bolivia, Australia and other mining regions; commonly associated with lead-zinc, copper and gold ores.
- **Natural form:** Native metal in some deposits, but much silver is bound in minerals or recovered as a by-product.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Mined directly or recovered from other metal ores, then refined. Used in electronics, solar panels, jewelry, mirrors and specialized antimicrobial applications.

### 48. Cadmium (Cd)
- **Where in nature:** Small concentrations in sphalerite, the main zinc ore, across zinc-mining regions worldwide.
- **Natural form:** Usually substitutes for zinc in minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered mainly as a by-product of zinc refining. Used in some batteries, pigments and specialized solar cells. Cadmium is toxic and its uses are restricted.

### 49. Indium (In)
- **Where in nature:** Trace amounts in zinc sulfide ores in China, South Korea, Canada and other zinc-producing regions.
- **Natural form:** Dispersed in zinc minerals, not usually found in concentrated indium deposits.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during zinc refining. Indium tin oxide is used in transparent conductive coatings for touchscreens and displays.

### 50. Tin (Sn)
- **Where in nature:** Cassiterite deposits in Indonesia, Malaysia, China, Myanmar, Bolivia and Cornwall in England; cassiterite can occur in granite-related veins and in alluvial stream sediments.
- **Natural form:** Mainly cassiterite (tin dioxide); native tin is rare.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Cassiterite is concentrated and reduced to tin metal. Used in solder, tin-plated steel, bronze and other alloys.

### 51. Antimony (Sb)
- **Where in nature:** Stibnite-bearing veins and deposits in China, Tajikistan, Türkiye and other mineral-producing regions.
- **Natural form:** Mainly stibnite (antimony sulfide) and related minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from ores and refined. Used in some flame-retardant formulations, alloys, lead-acid batteries and specialized electronics. Toxicity and environmental risks require controls.

### 52. Tellurium (Te)
- **Where in nature:** Tiny amounts in telluride minerals and copper-refining residues; some sources are associated with copper deposits and processing in China, Japan, North America and elsewhere.
- **Natural form:** Telluride minerals or trace substitutions in sulfide ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered mainly as a copper-refining by-product. Used in cadmium telluride solar cells, thermoelectric materials and specialized alloys.

### 53. Iodine (I)
- **Where in nature:** Caliche deposits in northern Chile's Atacama region; iodine-rich underground brines in Japan; small quantities in seawater, seaweed and marine organisms.
- **Natural form:** Iodide or iodate compounds in brines, sediments and organisms, not generally as free iodine crystals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from brines or caliche. Used in iodized salt, antiseptics, pharmaceuticals, medical contrast agents and chemical manufacturing.

### 54. Xenon (Xe)
- **Where in nature:** Trace amounts in Earth's atmosphere; no concentrated ordinary mineral deposits.
- **Natural form:** Monatomic noble gas.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from air-separation plants. Used in specialized lamps, ion propulsion, scientific equipment and certain medical applications.

### 55. Caesium (Cs)
- **Where in nature:** Pollucite-bearing granitic pegmatites, including the Tanco deposit in Manitoba, Canada, and other rare mineral deposits.
- **Natural form:** Caesium ions in rare aluminosilicate minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from pollucite and refined. Used in atomic clocks, research instruments and specialized electronic or vacuum applications.

### 56. Barium (Ba)
- **Where in nature:** Barite deposits in China, India, Morocco and the United States; witherite occurs in some mineral veins.
- **Natural form:** Mainly barite (barium sulfate) and witherite (barium carbonate).
- **Obtaining and uses:** Barite is mined and processed into barium compounds. Barium sulfate is used as an X-ray contrast agent and in drilling fluids. Soluble barium compounds can be toxic.

### 57. Lanthanum (La)
- **Where in nature:** Bastnäsite deposits at Bayan Obo in China and Mountain Pass in California; monazite in mineral sands in Australia, India and Brazil.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with other rare-earth elements in minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth processing. Used in optical glass, catalysts, battery alloys and specialized ceramics.

### 58. Cerium (Ce)
- **Where in nature:** Bastnäsite and monazite in China, the United States, Australia, India and Brazil. Cerium is relatively abundant among rare-earth elements.
- **Natural form:** Rare-earth minerals, not native metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during rare-earth separation. Used in glass-polishing powders, catalysts, lighter flints and specialty materials.

### 59. Praseodymium (Pr)
- **Where in nature:** Mixed rare-earth minerals such as bastnäsite and monazite in China, Australia, the United States and Brazil.
- **Natural form:** Occurs with other lanthanides in mineral ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth refining. Used in permanent magnets, specialty glass, pigments and high-performance alloys.

### 60. Neodymium (Nd)
- **Where in nature:** Bastnäsite and monazite deposits in China, Australia, the United States and other rare-earth-producing regions.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with other rare-earth elements in minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth refining. Used in powerful permanent magnets for motors, headphones, speakers, hard drives and wind turbines.

## Part IV — Elements 61–80

### 61. Promethium (Pm)
- **Where in nature:** Extremely tiny, short-lived traces may form through natural nuclear processes, but there are no meaningful natural deposits.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes; promethium has no stable isotope.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced mainly in nuclear reactors. Used in specialized research and certain niche nuclear-powered devices.

### 62. Samarium (Sm)
- **Where in nature:** Monazite and bastnäsite deposits in China, Australia, India, Brazil and other rare-earth regions.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with other lanthanides in minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth processing. Used in samarium-cobalt permanent magnets, specialized nuclear applications and research.

### 63. Europium (Eu)
- **Where in nature:** Rare-earth mineral deposits such as Bayan Obo in China and Mountain Pass in California, usually mixed with other lanthanides.
- **Natural form:** A minor constituent of rare-earth ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during rare-earth separation. Europium compounds provide red and blue phosphors in displays and lighting and are used in some security markings.

### 64. Gadolinium (Gd)
- **Where in nature:** Monazite, bastnäsite and other rare-earth minerals in China, Australia, India and the United States.
- **Natural form:** Mixed rare-earth mineral constituent.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated from rare-earth mixtures. Gadolinium compounds are used in certain MRI contrast agents, neutron-absorbing materials and specialized magnetic applications.

### 65. Terbium (Tb)
- **Where in nature:** Rare-earth mineral ores and some ion-adsorption clay deposits, especially in southern China.
- **Natural form:** Trace component of mixed rare-earth minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered through rare-earth separation. Used in green phosphors, specialized magnets and electronic materials.

### 66. Dysprosium (Dy)
- **Where in nature:** Rare-earth minerals and ion-adsorption clays in southern China and other rare-earth-bearing regions.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with other lanthanides.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth processing. Used in some permanent magnets to help them retain magnetic performance at elevated temperatures.

### 67. Holmium (Ho)
- **Where in nature:** Monazite, xenotime and other rare-earth minerals in China, Australia, India and elsewhere.
- **Natural form:** Minor constituent of rare-earth ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered through rare-earth separation. Used in specialized lasers, magnetic research and certain nuclear applications.

### 68. Erbium (Er)
- **Where in nature:** Xenotime, monazite and other rare-earth minerals in China, Australia, India and Brazil.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with other lanthanides in ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth refining. Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers strengthen optical signals in telecommunications; erbium is also used in lasers and optical glass.

### 69. Thulium (Tm)
- **Where in nature:** Trace quantities in monazite, xenotime and other rare-earth minerals around the world.
- **Natural form:** Minor constituent of rare-earth ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth refining. Used in specialized lasers, research and certain portable X-ray sources.

### 70. Ytterbium (Yb)
- **Where in nature:** Xenotime, monazite and other rare-earth minerals in China, Australia, India and other mineral-producing countries.
- **Natural form:** Mixed with other lanthanides in minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered through rare-earth separation. Used in fiber lasers, atomic-clock research, specialized alloys and scientific instruments.

### 71. Lutetium (Lu)
- **Where in nature:** Small amounts in xenotime and other rare-earth minerals in Australia, China, Brazil and elsewhere.
- **Natural form:** Minor component of rare-earth ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during rare-earth processing. Lutetium-containing crystals are used in PET scanner detectors; lutetium compounds also have catalyst and medical-isotope applications.

### 72. Hafnium (Hf)
- **Where in nature:** Usually mixed with zirconium in zircon-bearing heavy-mineral sands in Australia, South Africa and India.
- **Natural form:** Hafnium substitutes for zirconium in zircon and related minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Separated during zirconium refining because the two elements have very similar chemistry. Used in high-k microchip materials, control components and high-temperature alloys.

### 73. Tantalum (Ta)
- **Where in nature:** Tantalite and columbite-tantalite ores in Brazil, Australia, Rwanda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and other regions.
- **Natural form:** Oxide minerals in rare-element pegmatites and related deposits.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from tantalum-bearing minerals. Used in compact capacitors, corrosion-resistant equipment, specialized alloys and medical implants.

### 74. Tungsten (W)
- **Where in nature:** Wolframite and scheelite deposits in China, Vietnam, Bolivia, Portugal and other mining regions; often associated with granite-related veins.
- **Natural form:** Tungsten oxide minerals, not native metal.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Ores are concentrated and chemically processed. Used in hard cutting tools, wear-resistant parts, high-temperature components and electrical contacts.

### 75. Rhenium (Re)
- **Where in nature:** Tiny concentrations in molybdenite, especially in porphyry copper-molybdenum deposits in Chile, the United States and other mining regions.
- **Natural form:** Trace substitution in molybdenite and related ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered as a by-product of molybdenum processing. Used in jet-engine superalloys and petroleum-refining catalysts.

### 76. Osmium (Os)
- **Where in nature:** Platinum-group ores and certain nickel-copper deposits in South Africa, Russia and Canada; can occur in natural platinum-group alloy grains.
- **Natural form:** Very rare metal associated with platinum-group minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during platinum-group metal refining. Used in specialized research and some exceptionally wear-resistant alloys. Some osmium compounds are highly hazardous.

### 77. Iridium (Ir)
- **Where in nature:** Platinum-group deposits in South Africa and Russia; also in meteorites and in certain geological layers enriched by ancient impact events.
- **Natural form:** Rare metal mixed with platinum-group ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered during platinum-group metal refining. Used in specialized electrodes, spark-plug components, high-temperature equipment and catalysts.

### 78. Platinum (Pt)
- **Where in nature:** Platinum-group deposits in South Africa's Bushveld Complex, Russia's Norilsk region and elsewhere; small grains can occur in placer sediments.
- **Natural form:** Native platinum and platinum-group alloys, plus platinum-bearing minerals.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Mined and refined from platinum-group ores. Used in catalytic converters, jewelry, chemical catalysts, laboratory equipment and medical devices.

### 79. Gold (Au)
- **Where in nature:** Quartz veins and altered rocks in Egypt's Eastern Desert, including the wider Sukari and Hamash mining regions; placer deposits in river gravels; major gold regions in South Africa, Australia, China, Russia and North America.
- **Natural form:** Often native metal or a gold-silver alloy, sometimes microscopic particles in ore. Gold can be liberated from rock by weathering and concentrated in stream sediments.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Extracted from rock or sediment and refined. Used in jewelry, electronics, corrosion-resistant contacts, specialized coatings and monetary reserves.

### 80. Mercury (Hg)
- **Where in nature:** Cinnabar-bearing deposits, historically important at Almadén in Spain, Idrija in Slovenia and in China; small amounts also occur in volcanic emissions, soils and rocks.
- **Natural form:** Mainly cinnabar (mercury sulfide); native liquid mercury is uncommon.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Historically extracted from cinnabar. Modern use is restricted in many applications due to toxicity. It remains in some controlled scientific and industrial applications.

## Part V — Elements 81–100

### 81. Thallium (Tl)
- **Where in nature:** Tiny amounts in sulfide ores, especially some lead-zinc deposits, and in certain volcanic or geothermal environments.
- **Natural form:** Trace constituent of other minerals; not usually mined from a dedicated thallium ore.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered as a by-product of metal refining. Used in limited specialized electronics, optical materials and research. Thallium compounds are highly toxic.

### 82. Lead (Pb)
- **Where in nature:** Galena deposits in Australia, China, Peru, the United States and other regions; lower concentrations occur in soils, rocks and sediments.
- **Natural form:** Mainly galena (lead sulfide), with other lead minerals in oxidized deposits.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Lead ores are mined and refined; recycled lead is also important. Used in lead-acid batteries, radiation shielding and specialized industrial applications. Lead is toxic, particularly to children.

### 83. Bismuth (Bi)
- **Where in nature:** Bismuth-bearing veins and ores in China, Bolivia, Vietnam and other metal-producing regions, often associated with lead, tin, tungsten and copper deposits.
- **Natural form:** Bismuth minerals and, less commonly, native bismuth.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from ores or metal-refining by-products. Used in some medicines, low-melting alloys and specialized manufacturing.

### 84. Polonium (Po)
- **Where in nature:** Minute quantities in uranium-bearing rocks and ores, produced as part of radioactive decay chains.
- **Natural form:** Short-lived radioactive isotopes at extremely low concentrations.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Requires specialized nuclear or radiochemical production. Its uses are highly restricted because of its extreme radioactivity.

### 85. Astatine (At)
- **Where in nature:** Transient atoms formed in radioactive decay chains in uranium- and thorium-bearing rocks. The amount present at any moment is extraordinarily small.
- **Natural form:** Short-lived radioactive isotopes; no stable isotope exists.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced in tiny quantities in particle accelerators. Studied for nuclear chemistry and experimental targeted alpha therapy.

### 86. Radon (Rn)
- **Where in nature:** Produced when radium in uranium-bearing rocks and soils decays. It can seep into buildings through foundations and accumulate in basements, caves and underground spaces. Indoor levels vary greatly with local geology and ventilation.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive noble gas.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Usually monitored rather than collected for use. Radon exposure is a significant lung-cancer risk; testing and mitigation matter in areas where it accumulates.

### 87. Francium (Fr)
- **Where in nature:** Extremely rare, short-lived atoms produced in radioactive decay chains in uranium-bearing materials.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive atoms that decay rapidly; no stable isotope exists.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Studied in specialized nuclear and atomic-physics experiments. There is no commercial bulk use.

### 88. Radium (Ra)
- **Where in nature:** Trace amounts in uranium-bearing minerals and rocks, including some uraninite deposits.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive decay product present at very low concentrations.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Historically isolated from uranium ores. Modern uses are limited to tightly controlled research and certain specialized medical applications; radium is hazardous.

### 89. Actinium (Ac)
- **Where in nature:** Tiny traces in uranium ores as a product of radioactive decay.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes; no stable isotope exists.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced or separated through specialized nuclear processing. Actinium-225 is studied and used in targeted alpha therapy for certain cancers under medical controls.

### 90. Thorium (Th)
- **Where in nature:** Monazite-bearing beach sands and heavy-mineral deposits in India, Australia, Brazil and other regions; also in some granites and thorium minerals.
- **Natural form:** Mainly thorium dioxide and thorium-bearing minerals; thorium-232 is long-lived but radioactive.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Recovered from thorium-bearing minerals. Used in nuclear research and investigated as a potential nuclear fuel; some specialized alloys and historical gas mantles also used thorium compounds.

### 91. Protactinium (Pa)
- **Where in nature:** Tiny quantities in uranium-bearing minerals, generated during radioactive decay.
- **Natural form:** Rare radioactive isotopes dispersed in ores.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Obtained only through specialized radiochemical or nuclear processing. Used mainly in scientific research.

### 92. Uranium (U)
- **Where in nature:** Uraninite and other uranium minerals in Canada, Kazakhstan, Australia, Namibia and elsewhere; sandstone-hosted deposits in several countries. Uranium-bearing rocks and occurrences are also documented in parts of Egypt's Eastern Desert and Sinai.
- **Natural form:** Uranium oxides and other minerals; uranium occurs in trace amounts in many rocks and some waters.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Uranium ore is mined and processed under strict environmental and radiation controls. Certain isotopes are used as nuclear fuel; uranium is also used in research and geological dating.

### 93. Neptunium (Np)
- **Where in nature:** Minute natural traces can arise through nuclear reactions, but no meaningful natural deposits exist.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes; most available neptunium is human-produced.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced mainly in nuclear reactors. Used in nuclear science and as a precursor to certain specialized isotopes.

### 94. Plutonium (Pu)
- **Where in nature:** Extremely tiny natural traces may occur in uranium ores, but almost all available plutonium is produced in nuclear reactors.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes formed by nuclear reactions.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced through controlled nuclear processes. Used in tightly regulated nuclear technology and certain radioisotope power sources for space missions.

### 95. Americium (Am)
- **Where in nature:** No significant natural deposits; produced artificially through nuclear reactions.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Produced in nuclear reactors. Americium-241 is used in some ionization-type smoke detectors and specialized industrial measurement devices.

### 96. Curium (Cm)
- **Where in nature:** No meaningful natural deposits; produced in nuclear reactors and specialized nuclear facilities.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Used mainly for scientific research and specialized radiation-source applications.

### 97. Berkelium (Bk)
- **Where in nature:** No meaningful natural deposits; created artificially in nuclear reactors or other specialized facilities.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Used in nuclear research, including the synthesis of heavier elements.

### 98. Californium (Cf)
- **Where in nature:** No significant natural deposits; produced artificially.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Certain isotopes provide neutron sources for specialized industrial analysis, reactor start-up and scientific research.

### 99. Einsteinium (Es)
- **Where in nature:** Not found in ordinary natural deposits. It has been produced in extremely small quantities through intense nuclear reactions.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Used in fundamental nuclear research and experiments that help scientists produce heavier elements.

### 100. Fermium (Fm)
- **Where in nature:** Not found in meaningful natural deposits. It is produced through nuclear reactions and has been identified in tiny quantities in specialized experiments.
- **Natural form:** Radioactive isotopes.
- **Obtaining and uses:** Used only for fundamental research into heavy nuclei and nuclear stability.

## Egypt: selected natural-occurrence examples

These examples connect the guide to local geology. They describe known geological settings, not a claim that every site is an active mine or that the element is recoverable everywhere.

- **Gold:** gold-bearing quartz veins and altered rocks in the Eastern Desert, including the broader Sukari and Hamash regions.
- **Iron:** iron-bearing deposits including the Bahariya Oasis region.
- **Copper:** copper-bearing mineralization in parts of Sinai and the Eastern Desert.
- **Manganese:** deposits documented in Sinai.
- **Phosphate:** deposits including Abu Tartur and parts of the Red Sea region.
- **Silica:** silica-rich sands and quartz-bearing materials in Sinai and around the Gulf of Suez.
- **Calcium:** extensive limestone formations along the Nile Valley and in desert regions.
- **Uranium:** uranium-bearing geological occurrences have been documented in parts of the Eastern Desert and Sinai; an occurrence does not automatically mean an economic resource or active mine.
- **Sodium and chlorine:** halite, saline lakes and brines in evaporative basins and northern coastal lagoons.

## Why elements gather in particular places

A few geological mechanisms explain many mineral deposits:

- **Cooling magma:** as molten rock cools, different minerals crystallize at different times. Some rare elements concentrate in the remaining melt, producing pegmatites rich in minerals such as spodumene, beryl, tantalite or pollucite.
- **Hot fluids moving through rock:** hydrothermal fluids dissolve and transport metals, then deposit minerals when temperature, pressure or chemistry changes. This can create gold, copper, silver and other metal veins.
- **Weathering in warm, wet climates:** intense weathering can remove some soluble material and leave aluminium-rich bauxite or iron-rich laterite behind.
- **Sedimentation:** minerals can settle in ancient seas or basins, creating iron formations, phosphate deposits and layers of salt.
- **Evaporation:** when saline water evaporates, dissolved ions become concentrated and can crystallize as halite, gypsum, potash salts or other evaporite minerals.
- **Placer concentration:** flowing water or waves sort grains by density, concentrating heavy minerals and sometimes gold in stream beds and beach sands.
- **Biological cycling:** organisms concentrate elements into structures or molecules. Examples include carbon in wood, calcium in shells and bones, iodine in some marine organisms, and phosphorus in DNA and phosphate minerals.
- **Radioactive decay:** unstable nuclei transform into other nuclei. This produces helium, radon and tiny traces of several rare radioactive elements.
- **Nuclear synthesis:** particle accelerators and reactors create isotopes or elements that are too unstable or too scarce to occur as accessible natural deposits.

## A crucial distinction: element, mineral, ore and resource

- **Element:** a substance defined by its number of protons, such as iron or copper.
- **Mineral:** a naturally occurring solid with a characteristic composition and structure, such as hematite (iron oxide) or quartz (silicon dioxide).
- **Ore:** rock or mineral material from which a useful substance can be extracted economically under particular conditions.
- **Deposit:** a geological concentration of a material; it may or may not be economically recoverable.
- **Resource and reserve:** resource is a broader estimate of material with potential value; reserve refers to the economically recoverable part under stated technical, economic and legal conditions.

For example, aluminium is abundant in Earth's crust, but only some aluminium-rich material qualifies as bauxite ore. Quartz is common, but semiconductor-grade silicon requires extensive processing and purification.
