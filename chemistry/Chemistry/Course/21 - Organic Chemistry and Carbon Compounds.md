# Organic Chemistry and Carbon Compounds

Organic chemistry studies carbon-containing compounds, especially those built around carbon frameworks. Some carbon compounds, such as carbon dioxide and carbonates, are often treated separately in introductory courses, so “organic” is a useful convention rather than a perfect definition.

## Why carbon is versatile

Carbon has four valence electrons and commonly forms four covalent bonds. It can bond to itself, forming chains, branches, rings, and multiple bonds, and it can bond to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and halogens. This creates an enormous variety of structures.

The same formula can sometimes represent different structures. These are **isomers**. Structural isomers differ in which atoms are connected; stereoisomers share connectivity but differ in three-dimensional arrangement.

## Functional groups

A functional group is a recurring arrangement of atoms that strongly influences a molecule's properties and reactions. Common examples:
- alcohol: –OH attached to a carbon framework;
- aldehyde: terminal carbonyl group;
- ketone: carbonyl within a carbon framework;
- carboxylic acid: –COOH;
- amine: nitrogen-containing group;
- ester: –COO– linkage;
- alkene: carbon-carbon double bond;
- alkyne: carbon-carbon triple bond.

These labels are not enough to predict every reaction, but they organize a large subject into reusable patterns.

## Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen.
- Alkanes contain only single carbon-carbon bonds in their usual open-chain form.
- Alkenes contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond.
- Alkynes contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond.
- Aromatic compounds include systems such as benzene, whose electrons are delocalized around a ring.

The simple formula patterns for straight-chain alkanes, alkenes, and alkynes assume particular structures and no rings or additional unsaturation; do not apply them blindly to every molecule.

## Structure controls properties

Chain length, branching, functional groups, polarity, and shape influence boiling point, solubility, and reactivity. Small alcohols can interact strongly with water through hydrogen bonding; long hydrocarbon chains are nonpolar and often poorly soluble in water.

A formula alone may not determine a compound's properties. Connectivity and three-dimensional shape matter.

## Polymers

A polymer is a large molecule built from repeating structural units. Natural examples include proteins, cellulose, and DNA; synthetic examples include polyethylene and nylon. Polymer properties depend on chain structure, branching, crosslinking, crystallinity, and intermolecular forces.

## Biochemistry connection

Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids are major biological molecule classes. Their chemistry follows the same principles of bonding, polarity, acid-base behavior, reaction energetics, and molecular shape learned elsewhere in this course. Biology adds complexity through large structures, water-rich environments, and enzyme-catalyzed pathways.
