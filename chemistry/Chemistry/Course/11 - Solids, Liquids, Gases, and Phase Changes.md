# Solids, Liquids, Gases, and Phase Changes

The state of a substance depends on particle motion, interactions, pressure, and temperature. It is not simply that solids have no motion and gases have no attraction.

## Solid

In a typical solid, particles occupy positions within a structure and vibrate around them. The material keeps its shape and volume under ordinary conditions. Some solids are crystalline, with repeating long-range order; others are amorphous, with no long-range repeating arrangement.

Metallic solids, ionic crystals, molecular crystals, and covalent-network solids have different bonding structures and properties. “Solid” describes a state, not one particular kind of chemical bond.

## Liquid

In a liquid, particles remain close together but can rearrange. A liquid has a nearly fixed volume under ordinary pressure but takes the shape of its container. Attractions are significant, but they do not lock every particle into one permanent position.

Viscosity measures resistance to flow. It depends on temperature, molecular shape, and interactions. Surface tension arises because particles at a surface have a different set of neighboring interactions than particles in the bulk.

## Gas

Gas particles are usually much farther apart than in liquids and move through the available volume. A gas expands to fill its container and is compressible because much of its volume is empty space between particles. Collisions with container walls create pressure.

The ideal-gas model treats particles as having negligible volume and no intermolecular forces except during collisions. It works well at relatively low pressure and high temperature, but real gases deviate when particles are crowded or attractions become important.

## Temperature and phase

Increasing temperature generally changes the distribution of particle energies. More particles can overcome barriers to rearrangement or escape from neighboring attractions. Temperature is not a direct measure of “how fast every particle moves”; it relates to an average and a distribution.

A phase change can occur when the system's free-energy balance favors a different arrangement. At a fixed pressure, melting and boiling happen at characteristic temperatures for a pure substance, though impurities and pressure can shift them.

## Six common phase changes

- melting: solid → liquid;
- freezing: liquid → solid;
- vaporization: liquid → gas;
- condensation: gas → liquid;
- sublimation: solid → gas;
- deposition: gas → solid.

During a phase transition at equilibrium, energy may change the arrangement rather than temperature. Latent heat describes the energy transfer associated with a phase change.

## Evaporation versus boiling

**Evaporation** happens at the surface and can occur below the boiling point. Some surface molecules have enough energy to escape into the gas. The remaining liquid may cool because the escaping molecules carry away energy.

**Boiling** occurs when vapor bubbles can form and grow throughout the liquid. The boiling point depends on pressure. Water boils at a lower temperature at lower atmospheric pressure because its vapor pressure reaches the surrounding pressure sooner.

## Vapor pressure and dynamic equilibrium

In a closed container, some liquid molecules enter the vapor while some vapor molecules return to the liquid. At dynamic equilibrium, the rates of evaporation and condensation are equal, even though both continue. The vapor pressure depends strongly on temperature.

A volatile liquid has a relatively high vapor pressure at a given temperature. Volatility is not the same as flammability: a substance can evaporate readily without being highly flammable.

## Water and ice

Ice is less dense than liquid water at ordinary pressure because hydrogen bonding forms an open crystal structure. When ice melts, some of this ordered structure collapses and molecules can pack more closely. This is unusual; many substances become denser when they freeze.

Water's phase behavior affects weather, oceans, geology, and living systems.
