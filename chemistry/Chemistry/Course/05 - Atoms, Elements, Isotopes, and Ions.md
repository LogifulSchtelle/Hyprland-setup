# Atoms, Elements, Isotopes, and Ions

## What makes an atom an atom?

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons described by quantum states. The nucleus contains protons and, in most atoms, neutrons. Almost all the atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus, while the electron distribution occupies most of its spatial size.

An **element** is defined by proton number. Every hydrogen nucleus has one proton; every carbon nucleus has six; every oxygen nucleus has eight. Change the proton count and you have a different element.

This gives the atomic number, Z:

\[
Z = \text{number of protons}
\]

For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons. Neutrality is a charge balance, not a definition of the element.

## Isotopes: same element, different nucleus

An **isotope** is a version of an element with a particular number of neutrons. Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons; carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons. Both are carbon because both have six protons.

The mass number A is the total number of protons and neutrons:

\[
A = Z + N
\]

where N is neutron number. Thus N = A − Z. The notation carbon-14 or ¹⁴C means the nucleus has a mass number of 14, not that each atom weighs exactly 14 grams.

Isotopes usually have very similar chemical behavior because chemical bonding is governed primarily by electrons, and isotopes of a neutral element have the same electron count. They can differ in mass-dependent behavior and nuclear stability. Some isotopes are stable; others are radioactive and transform over time.

## Why neutrons matter

Protons all have positive charge and repel one another electrically. The nucleus stays bound because the strong nuclear interaction is attractive at very short distances. Neutrons contribute to nuclear binding without adding electric repulsion. The balance of nuclear forces and quantum structure determines which nuclei are stable.

This is a nuclear-physics story, distinct from ordinary chemical bonding. Chemical reactions generally rearrange electrons and nuclei as intact units; nuclear reactions alter nuclei themselves.

## Ions: changing electrons, not identity

An atom becomes an ion when it gains or loses electrons:
- losing electrons produces a positive ion (cation);
- gaining electrons produces a negative ion (anion).

For a particle with proton number Z and electron number E, net charge in units of e is Z − E. For example, 11 protons and 10 electrons gives a +1 charge, written Na⁺ if the nucleus is sodium.

A charge is not a label that an atom “wants.” It follows from the count of positive and negative charges. Ions form when the full system's energy and environment make electron transfer or redistribution favorable, often in combination with other interactions.

## Atomic mass and the periodic table

The periodic table often lists an average atomic mass that is not a whole number. For example, a natural sample contains isotopes in particular proportions, and the listed value is a weighted average of their isotopic masses. It is not the mass number of one particular atom.

A weighted average is found by multiplying each isotope's mass by its fractional abundance and adding the results. If an element had two isotopes of masses 10 and 11 with abundances 20% and 80%, its average would be 0.20(10) + 0.80(11) = 10.8.

## Electron configuration is the chemical bridge

Changing proton number changes the nuclear charge and the element. Changing neutron number changes isotope. Changing electron number changes charge. Changing how electrons occupy quantum states can change chemical reactivity and bonding.

These are four distinct ideas:
- element identity → proton number;
- isotope identity → neutron number for that element;
- ion charge → proton-electron imbalance;
- chemical behavior → especially electron arrangement and interactions.
