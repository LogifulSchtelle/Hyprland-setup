# Matter, Particles, and Classification

## What is matter?

In introductory chemistry, **matter** is physical stuff with mass and volume. Air, water, a rock, and your body are matter. Light is energy carried by electromagnetic radiation, not matter in the same everyday classification. Modern physics has subtleties about fields and mass-energy, but the introductory distinction is useful.

A **system** is the part of the world we choose to study. If we study water in a cup, the water may be our system and the cup and room its surroundings. Defining the system helps us track matter and energy entering or leaving it.

## Particles as a useful model

Many substances can be described in terms of atoms, molecules, or ions:
- **Atom:** a unit of an element.
- **Molecule:** a defined group of atoms held together, such as H₂O.
- **Ion:** a particle with net electric charge, such as Na⁺ or Cl⁻.

Particles are not all identical little hard spheres. The particle model is a starting point; quantum mechanics provides a more accurate account of electrons and bonding.

Between particles there can be empty space relative to their size, and particles are in motion. In a solid, particles do not usually sit perfectly motionless; they vibrate and interact. In a liquid, particles can rearrange while remaining close together. In a gas, particles are generally much farther apart and move freely between collisions.

## Pure substances and mixtures

A **pure substance** has a fixed chemical identity and composition. It may be an element or a compound.
- An **element** contains atoms with the same number of protons.
- A **compound** contains two or more elements chemically combined in a definite ratio.

A **mixture** contains substances physically combined without requiring one fixed chemical formula. Air is a mixture of gases; salt water is a mixture of water and dissolved ions.

Mixtures can be:
- **Homogeneous:** composition is uniform at the scale being considered, like well-mixed salt water.
- **Heterogeneous:** composition varies from place to place, like oil and water or sand in water.

“Uniform” depends on scale. Milk looks uniform to the eye but is a colloid with dispersed droplets and particles.

## Physical and chemical properties

A **physical property** can be observed without changing a substance's chemical identity: density, melting point, color, conductivity, or solubility.

A **chemical property** describes how a substance can undergo chemical change: iron's tendency to oxidize or a fuel's ability to react with oxygen.

A **physical change** changes state, shape, or distribution without changing the identity of the chemical species, as when ice melts. A **chemical change** rearranges atoms into different chemical species, as when hydrogen and oxygen react to form water.

The boundary can require care. Dissolving salt in water is usually treated as a physical process in basic chemistry because hydrated ions can be recovered by evaporating the water. But dissolution involves real ion-water interactions and can involve energy changes. “Physical” does not mean “nothing happened.”

## Conservation of matter

In an ordinary chemical reaction in a closed system, atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed. Therefore, total mass remains constant to the precision of ordinary chemical measurements. If a reaction seems to lose mass, a gas may have escaped or matter may have entered from outside.

Example: when vinegar reacts with baking soda in an open container, carbon dioxide gas escapes. The container and remaining material may weigh less afterward, but if we include the escaped gas, the total matter is accounted for.

At the nuclear level, mass can convert to other forms of energy, so the deeper conservation law is mass-energy conservation. Chemical energy changes are tiny compared with the rest-mass energy of the particles, so ordinary chemistry uses conservation of mass with excellent accuracy.

## Separating mixtures

Separation methods exploit differences in physical properties:
- **Filtration:** particle size and insolubility.
- **Evaporation/crystallization:** differences in volatility and solubility.
- **Distillation:** differences in volatility or boiling behavior.
- **Chromatography:** different attractions to a stationary phase and a moving phase.
- **Decanting:** settling and density differences.
- **Centrifugation:** accelerated settling of components with different densities or sizes.

Separation does not normally change a component's chemical identity. It sorts existing substances.

## Why this matters

Before explaining a reaction, first ask what is in the sample. Is it a pure compound or a mixture? Is it one phase or several? Are we observing a physical change or new chemical species? Many mistakes begin with classifying the situation incorrectly.
