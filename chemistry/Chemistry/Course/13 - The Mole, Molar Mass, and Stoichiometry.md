# The Mole, Molar Mass, and Stoichiometry

Chemists count unimaginably many particles by weighing samples. The bridge between a microscopic count and a laboratory mass is the mole.

## Why a counting unit is needed

Atoms are far too small to count one by one in a normal laboratory. A **mole** is a defined amount containing exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. This number is the Avogadro constant when expressed per mole.

The entity must be named: one mole of water molecules, sodium ions, electrons, or carbon atoms. A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen, but vastly larger.

## Molar mass

**Molar mass** is the mass per mole, commonly expressed in g/mol. The periodic table's atomic mass values let us calculate approximate molar masses.

Water, H₂O:
- about 2 × 1.008 g/mol from hydrogen;
- about 16.00 g/mol from oxygen;
- total ≈ 18.02 g/mol.

This means 1 mol of water molecules has a mass of about 18.02 g. It does not mean one water molecule weighs 18 grams.

## The conversion triangle

The key relationships are:

\[
n = \frac{m}{M}, \qquad m=nM, \qquad N=nN_A
\]

- n: amount in moles;
- m: sample mass;
- M: molar mass;
- N: number of specified particles;
- N_A: Avogadro constant.

Example: how many moles are in 36.0 g of water? n = 36.0 g ÷ 18.02 g/mol ≈ 2.00 mol. Units cancel to mol.

## Chemical equations give mole ratios

The balanced equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O says that the stoichiometric ratio is 2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂ : 2 mol H₂O. It is not a mass ratio. Because oxygen molecules are heavier than hydrogen molecules, the mass amounts differ.

A calculation pathway:
1. Balance the equation.
2. Convert the given quantity to moles.
3. Use the coefficient ratio to find moles of the desired substance.
4. Convert to the requested unit, such as grams or particles.
5. Check units and whether the result makes sense.

## Worked example

How much water can form from 4.00 g H₂ if oxygen is in excess?

Molar mass H₂ ≈ 2.016 g/mol.
n(H₂) = 4.00 / 2.016 ≈ 1.984 mol.

From 2H₂ → 2H₂O, the ratio is 1:1, so n(H₂O) ≈ 1.984 mol.

Mass water ≈ 1.984 mol × 18.02 g/mol ≈ 35.8 g.

This result assumes the reaction proceeds completely and oxygen is available in excess. Real experiments can have incomplete conversion and losses.

## Limiting reactants

If both reactants are supplied in finite amounts, one may run out first. The **limiting reactant** determines the maximum product possible. The other reactant is in excess.

For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, compare available moles to the required 2:1 ratio. If you have 3 mol H₂ and 2 mol O₂, 3 mol H₂ needs only 1.5 mol O₂. Hydrogen is limiting, and oxygen remains in excess.

Do not identify the limiting reactant by comparing raw grams; convert to moles and use the balanced ratio.

## Theoretical and percent yield

The **theoretical yield** is the maximum product predicted from stoichiometry under the assumptions of the calculation. The **actual yield** is what is recovered in an experiment. Percent yield is:

\[
\%\text{ yield}=\frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}}\times100\%
\]

A low yield may arise from side reactions, incomplete reaction, product loss during separation, or measurement errors. A yield above 100% usually signals that the product contains impurities or solvent, or that measurement/calculation is wrong.

## Concentration: moles per volume

Molar concentration, or molarity, is:

\[
c=\frac{n}{V}
\]

where c is in mol/L when n is in mol and V is in litres. A 0.50 mol/L solution contains 0.50 mol of specified solute per litre of final solution. It does not mean 0.50 mol is added to one litre of solvent; the final volume matters.
