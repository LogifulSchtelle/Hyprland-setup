# Gases and the Ideal Gas Model

Gas laws connect pressure, volume, temperature, and amount. They work because many gas properties emerge from the motion and collisions of huge numbers of particles.

## Pressure from collisions

Pressure is force per unit area. Gas particles collide with container walls and transfer momentum; the collective effect produces pressure. More frequent or more forceful collisions can raise pressure.

The ideal-gas model assumes particles have negligible individual volume compared with the container and negligible intermolecular attractions except during collisions. It is most accurate at relatively low pressure and high temperature.

## Ideal gas equation

\[
PV=nRT
\]

P is pressure, V is volume, n is amount in moles, T is absolute temperature in kelvin, and R is the gas constant. Units must match the selected R value. For example, R ≈ 8.314 J/(mol·K) when pressure and volume combine to joules, or ≈ 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) for L and atm.

The equation says that, at fixed amount and temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related in the ideal model. At fixed pressure and amount, volume is proportional to absolute temperature. At fixed volume and temperature, pressure is proportional to amount.

## Why kelvin matters

Gas-law proportionality requires absolute temperature. Doubling Celsius from 20 °C to 40 °C does not double the Kelvin temperature. Convert first: 20 °C = 293.15 K and 40 °C = 313.15 K.

## Partial pressure

In a mixture of ideal gases, each gas contributes a **partial pressure**. Dalton's law says total pressure is the sum of partial pressures. For an ideal mixture, each gas's partial pressure is related to its mole fraction and total pressure.

This explains why air pressure is the combined effect of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, and other gases, not pressure from one component alone.

## Real gases

Real particles have finite size and attract one another. At high pressure, finite volume matters; at low temperature, attractions can become significant and gases may condense. More detailed equations, such as van der Waals-type models, correct some ideal-gas assumptions.

## Gas stoichiometry

If temperature and pressure are fixed and gases behave ideally, volume ratios follow mole ratios. In the balanced equation N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, one volume of nitrogen reacts with three volumes of hydrogen to form two volumes of ammonia gas, when measured at the same temperature and pressure and treated ideally.

The ratio follows from PV=nRT, not from gases having different-sized molecules in the equation.
