# Measurement, Models, and Scientific Reasoning

Chemistry is experimental: we compare ideas with observations. To do that well, we need careful measurement, clear units, and an understanding of what evidence can and cannot tell us.

## Observations versus explanations

An **observation** reports what was measured or noticed: a sample's mass is 12.4 g, a liquid turns blue, or a gas occupies 250 mL. An **explanation** proposes why it happened. “The liquid turned blue because an indicator changed form in this solution” is an explanation that can be tested.

A single observation rarely proves one unique explanation. Several models may explain the same result. More experiments help distinguish them.

A **hypothesis** is a testable proposed explanation. A **theory** in science is a broad, well-supported explanatory framework, not a casual guess. A **law** summarizes a regular pattern in nature; it may describe what happens without fully explaining why. These are different jobs, not simply levels on a ladder from weak to strong.

## Measurement and units

A measurement has a number and a unit. “5” alone is incomplete; 5 grams, 5 seconds, and 5 litres mean different things.

Common chemistry units:
- mass: gram (g), kilogram (kg)
- volume: litre (L), millilitre (mL), cubic centimetre (cm³)
- temperature: kelvin (K), degrees Celsius (°C)
- amount of substance: mole (mol)
- pressure: pascal (Pa), kilopascal (kPa), atmosphere (atm)
- energy: joule (J), kilojoule (kJ)

Useful conversions:
- 1 L = 1000 mL
- 1 mL = 1 cm³
- 1 kg = 1000 g
- temperature in kelvin: T(K) = temperature in °C + 273.15

Kelvin begins at absolute zero, the lowest thermodynamic temperature. It is not just Celsius with a different label. A temperature difference of 1 K is the same size as a difference of 1 °C, but the zero points differ.

## Significant figures and precision

A measurement is limited by the instrument and method. A balance reading 2.31 g communicates more precision than 2 g. **Precision** concerns the closeness of repeated measurements to one another; **accuracy** concerns closeness to the accepted value. A set of measurements can be precise but inaccurate if the instrument is systematically miscalibrated.

Do not report more digits than the measurement supports. Exact counted quantities, such as 12 eggs, differ from measured quantities, such as 12.0 g. Significant-figure rules are bookkeeping conventions for communicating uncertainty, not magic laws of nature.

## Density: a first equation

Density measures mass per unit volume:

\[
\rho = \frac{m}{V}
\]

Here ρ (rho) is density, m is mass, and V is volume. If 20 g of a substance occupies 10 cm³, its density is 2 g/cm³.

Rearrange only when the meaning is clear:
- m = ρV
- V = m/ρ

Density can help identify a material, but it is not always unique. Temperature, pressure, purity, and structure can change a sample's density.

## Dimensional analysis

Units can reveal mistakes before arithmetic does. Suppose speed = distance/time. Its units are metres per second (m/s). If a calculation produces metres times seconds, you likely used the relationship incorrectly.

In chemistry, conversion factors are ratios equal to one, such as 1000 mL / 1 L. Multiplying by this ratio changes the unit without changing the physical amount.

Example: 0.75 L × (1000 mL / 1 L) = 750 mL. The litre units cancel.

## Models and their limits

A model deliberately ignores some details to make a question manageable. Treating a gas as tiny particles moving randomly is excellent for explaining pressure and temperature. It is less accurate at high pressure or near condensation, where particle volume and attractions matter more.

A useful model should:
- explain known observations;
- make predictions;
- state or reveal its assumptions;
- be revised when evidence shows its limits.

## Graphs and relationships

A graph shows how two quantities vary together. A straight line through the origin often indicates direct proportionality: doubling one quantity doubles the other under the tested conditions. A curve indicates a different relationship. Correlation alone does not prove that one variable caused another; controlled experiments help separate causes.
