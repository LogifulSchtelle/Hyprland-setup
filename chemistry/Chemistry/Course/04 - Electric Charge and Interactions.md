# Electric Charge and Interactions

Chemistry depends heavily on electromagnetism. Atoms contain positively charged nuclei and negatively charged electrons. Their interactions help determine atomic sizes, bonds, molecular shapes, and material properties.

## Electric charge

There are two signs of electric charge, called positive and negative. Protons carry positive charge; electrons carry negative charge; neutrons have no net electric charge. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract.

The magnitude of the elementary charge is approximately 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb. A proton has +e and an electron has −e. An atom with equal numbers of protons and electrons has zero net charge, even though it contains charged parts.

Neutral does not mean “no electric forces.” It means the total charge adds to zero. Local charge distributions can still interact strongly.

## Coulomb's law, in plain language

For two point charges, the magnitude of the electric force grows with the product of the charge magnitudes and falls with the square of their separation:

\[
F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}
\]

Here F is force, q₁ and q₂ are charges, r is separation, and k is a constant for the medium. The equation's sign-independent form gives the magnitude; the signs tell us whether the interaction is attractive or repulsive.

Doubling the distance reduces the force to one quarter, all else equal. Doubling one charge doubles the force. This is an idealized point-charge relation; atoms have extended quantum charge distributions, so real chemical calculations require more sophisticated models.

## Why an atom can stay together

A nucleus is positive and electrons are negative, so electric attraction draws them toward one another. But electrons are not classical planets that can simply lose energy continuously and spiral into the nucleus. Quantum mechanics restricts the possible states, and the lowest allowed state has a finite spatial extent and energy.

A stable atom is a balance described by quantum mechanics and electromagnetic interaction, not a tiny solar system held together by miniature strings.

## Attraction is not the whole story

If attraction were the only consideration, oppositely charged particles might be expected to collapse without limit. But quantum rules, kinetic-energy effects, and the structure of allowed states matter. For molecules, repulsions matter too:
- electron-nucleus attraction;
- electron-electron repulsion;
- nucleus-nucleus repulsion;
- quantum restrictions on electron states.

A chemical bond is a stable arrangement of the full system. It is not explained by saying only “opposite charges attract.”

## Ions and charge balance

An **ion** is a particle with a net electric charge. A cation is positive; an anion is negative.
- If a neutral sodium atom loses one electron, it becomes Na⁺.
- If a neutral chlorine atom gains one electron, it becomes Cl⁻.

The nucleus does not need to change for an atom to become an ion. Changing electron number changes net charge; changing proton number changes the element itself.

In an ionic solid such as sodium chloride, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are arranged in a large repeating structure. It is misleading to picture every Na⁺ permanently paired with exactly one Cl⁻ molecule. The solid's stability comes from the collective arrangement of many ions.

## Polar molecules and partial charges

Even in a molecule with zero net charge, electrons may be distributed unevenly. A region can be partially negative (δ−) and another partially positive (δ+). This uneven distribution is called polarity.

Water is polar because its O–H bonds are polar and its bent geometry means their effects do not cancel. Polarity influences solubility, boiling points, and interactions between molecules.

## Why the electric story matters

The same basic interaction appears at many scales:
- electrons attracted to nuclei help atoms exist;
- nuclei and electrons interact when bonds form;
- partial charges influence molecular attractions;
- ions move in electric fields in solutions and batteries;
- electron transfer drives oxidation-reduction reactions.

Chemistry is not “only electricity,” but electromagnetic interactions are central to most ordinary chemical behavior.
