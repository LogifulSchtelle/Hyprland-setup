# Thermodynamics and Reaction Favorability

Thermodynamics asks which changes are energetically and statistically favored under specified conditions. It does not, by itself, tell us how fast a change happens.

## System and surroundings

The **system** is what we study; the **surroundings** are everything else. An open system exchanges matter and energy, a closed system exchanges energy but not matter, and an isolated system exchanges neither ideally.

Energy can cross the boundary as heat or work. A clear sign convention is essential when using equations.

## Internal energy and enthalpy

Internal energy U includes microscopic kinetic and potential energy. Its change follows an energy balance. At constant pressure, enthalpy H is especially useful:

\[
H=U+pV
\]

For a process at constant pressure with only pressure-volume work, ΔH corresponds to heat transferred into the system. Negative ΔH means the system releases heat to the surroundings under those conditions; positive ΔH means it absorbs heat.

## Entropy: counting accessible arrangements

**Entropy** is a state function connected to how energy and matter can be distributed among microscopic arrangements. A higher entropy is often associated with more accessible microstates, but “disorder” is only a rough analogy. Entropy is not simply messiness.

Gas formation often increases entropy because particles can occupy many more spatial arrangements. Mixing often increases entropy because there are more ways to distribute components. But entropy changes depend on the complete process and constraints.

## The second law

For a spontaneous process, the total entropy of an isolated universe (system plus surroundings) increases; at ideal reversible equilibrium, it is unchanged. A system's own entropy can decrease if the surroundings gain enough entropy.

This is why a crystal can form spontaneously from a solution under appropriate conditions: the system becomes more ordered, but the total entropy change, including the surroundings and energy transfers, can still favor the process.

## Gibbs free energy

At constant temperature and pressure, Gibbs free energy change is a useful criterion:

\[
\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S
\]

T is absolute temperature in kelvin; ΔH and TΔS must use compatible energy units. For a specified process:
- ΔG < 0: forward change is thermodynamically favorable under the stated conditions;
- ΔG > 0: the reverse direction is favored;
- ΔG = 0: equilibrium, with no net thermodynamic driving force.

“Favorable” does not mean “fast,” and ΔG is not a promise that every molecule will react. It describes the overall tendency under the given conditions.

## Temperature can change favorability

If ΔH is negative and ΔS positive, ΔG is negative at all positive temperatures in the simple constant-value approximation. If ΔH is positive and ΔS negative, the process is unfavorable at all temperatures under that approximation. If both are negative, lower temperature may favor the process; if both are positive, higher temperature may favor it.

Real ΔH and ΔS can vary with temperature, and phase changes can complicate this simplified pattern.

## Standard conditions and actual conditions

A standard free-energy change ΔG° refers to standard-state conventions. Actual free energy depends on composition:

\[
\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q
\]

R is the gas constant and Q is the reaction quotient. Q describes the current ratio of product activities to reactant activities, each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient. As composition changes, the direction of net change can change.

## Bond energies and reaction enthalpy

A useful approximate estimate is:
ΔH ≈ energy needed to break reactant bonds − energy released when product bonds form.

Breaking bonds costs energy; forming bonds releases energy. This estimate is approximate because average bond energies are usually gas-phase averages and do not fully capture every molecular environment, phase, or intermolecular contribution.
