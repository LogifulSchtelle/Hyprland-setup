# The Periodic Table and Valence Electrons

The periodic table organizes elements by atomic number and recurring chemical behavior. Its patterns emerge from nuclear charge and the quantum arrangement of electrons.

## Rows, columns, and blocks

A horizontal row is a **period**. A vertical column is a **group**. Elements in a group often share chemical patterns because they have related outer-electron arrangements. “Often” matters: trends have exceptions, especially among transition elements and heavier atoms.

The s-, p-, d-, and f-block labels reflect which type of subshell is being filled in the simplified electron-configuration picture. The block structure is a visible imprint of quantum rules.

## Valence electrons

**Valence electrons** are the electrons most involved in bonding and many chemical reactions. For main-group elements, the group position often gives a useful first estimate of the number of valence electrons:
- Group 1: usually 1;
- Group 2: usually 2;
- Groups 13–18: commonly 3–8, with helium as a special case having 2.

Transition metals are more complicated because d electrons can participate in bonding and several oxidation states are common.

Valence electrons do not mechanically “want” a full shell. Chemical behavior results from the energy of the entire system, including allowed electron states and interactions.

## Why periodic trends exist

Across a period, proton number increases. Added electrons enter the same broad shell in many main-group cases, while the stronger nuclear charge tends to pull electron density inward. This often increases **effective nuclear charge**, the net positive pull felt by an electron after accounting approximately for shielding by other electrons.

Down a group, outer electrons occupy shells farther from the nucleus and experience more shielding. This generally makes atoms larger.

These patterns help explain:
- atomic radius;
- ionization energy;
- electron affinity;
- electronegativity.

Each trend has nuances. Do not treat the periodic table as a perfect set of one-direction arrows.

## Atomic radius

Atomic radius is not a hard-edge measurement because electron probability distributions fade gradually. Different definitions use covalent, metallic, or van der Waals radii.

Broad trend: atomic size often decreases from left to right across a period and increases down a group. Across a period, effective nuclear attraction increases; down a group, added shells increase the distance of outer electron density.

## Ionization energy

**Ionization energy** is the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion. It usually rises across a period and falls down a group, broadly speaking. A tighter nuclear pull makes electron removal harder; a more distant and shielded outer electron is generally easier to remove.

There are exceptions due to subshell energies and electron pairing. The trend is a guide, not a substitute for the electron configuration.

## Electron affinity and electronegativity

**Electron affinity** describes the energy change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous species; sign conventions vary by source, so check how a table defines it.

**Electronegativity** describes how strongly an atom attracts shared electron density in a chemical bond. It is not the same as electron affinity. Fluorine is highly electronegative, so bonds to fluorine often have strongly polarized electron density.

## Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids

Metals often conduct electricity and heat, can be shaped, and tend to form cations in common chemical settings. Nonmetals often form covalent compounds and may gain electrons in ionic compounds. Metalloids have mixed properties and are important in semiconductors.

These are broad patterns, not absolute laws. Material properties depend on structure and conditions as well as element identity.

## Predicting simple ion charges

Main-group patterns often produce familiar ions: Group 1 elements form +1 ions, Group 2 +2, halogens often −1, and oxygen-family elements often −2 in simple ionic compounds. These patterns reflect energetic outcomes in particular compounds, not a universal rule that every atom must reach a noble-gas electron count.

The octet rule is a helpful introductory pattern for many main-group compounds, but it has exceptions. Hydrogen follows a duet pattern, electron-deficient compounds exist, radicals exist, and transition-metal chemistry needs more detailed models.
