# Reaction Rates and Activation Energy

Kinetics studies how fast reactions happen and how their rates respond to temperature, concentration, catalysts, and other conditions. Thermodynamics tells us about favorability; kinetics tells us about speed.

## Why favorable reactions can be slow

Reactants may need to pass through a high-energy arrangement called a **transition state** along a reaction pathway. The energy barrier between reactants and this transition state is the **activation energy** for that pathway.

A reaction can have products at lower free energy than reactants and still proceed slowly if few particles reach a suitable transition-state arrangement. A fuel and oxygen can coexist because initiating the reaction requires an effective pathway over an energy barrier.

## Temperature and energy distributions

Particles in a sample do not all have exactly the same energy. Their energies follow a distribution. Raising temperature increases the fraction of particles able to cross many activation barriers, often making reactions much faster.

The Arrhenius equation expresses a common temperature dependence:

\[
k=Ae^{-E_a/(RT)}
\]

k is a rate constant, A is a factor related to collision frequency and orientation, Eₐ is activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is kelvin. Because of the exponential, a moderate temperature rise can substantially increase rate.

## Collision theory, carefully used

A simple model says reacting particles must collide with suitable energy and orientation. More frequent collisions can increase rate, but not every collision reacts. The actual mechanism may involve multiple steps, solvent effects, surfaces, or quantum behavior beyond the simple picture.

## Concentration and rate laws

A rate law relates reaction rate to reactant concentrations, often in a form such as:
\[
\text{rate}=k[A]^m[B]^n
\]
The exponents m and n are determined experimentally for the overall rate law; they are not automatically the balanced-equation coefficients. For an elementary step, stoichiometry can relate to the rate law, but an overall multistep reaction is more complex.

Increasing concentration often increases collision frequency and rate, but the precise dependence is determined by mechanism and experiment.

## Catalysts

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation barrier. It changes how quickly equilibrium is reached but does not change the equilibrium constant for the same overall reaction at the same temperature. A catalyst does not change the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products.

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They can orient reactants and stabilize transition-state arrangements, speeding reactions under conditions compatible with life.

## Surface area and phase

A solid reactant may react faster when powdered because more surface is exposed to the other reactant. Heterogeneous catalysts work at surfaces, so surface structure and available active sites matter.

## Mechanisms and rate-determining steps

A reaction mechanism is a sequence of elementary steps. The observed rate can be controlled by a slow step or by a combination of steps and pre-equilibria. Mechanisms are hypotheses tested against kinetic data, intermediates, and other evidence.

## Safety connection

Heating, concentrating, or mixing reactive substances can dramatically change reaction rates. A reaction that is slow at room temperature may become dangerous when heated or when a catalyst is introduced. Do not perform experiments based on theoretical notes without suitable supervision, risk assessment, and lab procedures.
