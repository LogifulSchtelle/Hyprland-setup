# Solutions, Solubility, and Concentration

A solution is a homogeneous mixture at the scale being considered. The **solvent** is the component doing the dissolving; the **solute** is the component dispersed in it. In salt water, water is the solvent and sodium chloride is the solute.

## What dissolving means at particle level

When a substance dissolves, particles separate from their original arrangement and become surrounded by particles of the solvent. In water, NaCl separates into Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, which become hydrated by water molecules. Sugar dissolves mostly as intact molecules surrounded by water; it does not normally split into ions.

Dissolving requires overcoming some solute-solute and solvent-solvent interactions while creating new solute-solvent interactions. The energy balance and entropy change together determine whether mixing is favorable. Stirring can speed the process by moving fresh solvent to the surface, but stirring does not automatically increase the final equilibrium solubility.

## Solubility is not the same as speed

**Solubility** is how much solute can dissolve under specified conditions. **Dissolution rate** is how quickly it dissolves. Crushing a solid increases surface area and often speeds dissolving, but it does not necessarily change the equilibrium amount that can dissolve.

For many solids, solubility changes with temperature. Gas solubility in liquids often decreases as temperature rises, though pressure also matters. These are trends, not universal rules without exceptions.

## Saturated, unsaturated, and supersaturated

An **unsaturated** solution contains less dissolved solute than the equilibrium limit under its conditions. A **saturated** solution is at that limit and can coexist with undissolved solute. A **supersaturated** solution contains more dissolved solute than is stable at equilibrium and can crystallize when triggered.

At saturation, dissolving and crystallization can continue at equal rates. Dynamic equilibrium does not mean particles stop moving.

## Concentration units

Molarity is moles of solute per litre of solution:

\[
c=\frac{n}{V}
\]

If 0.20 mol solute is present in 0.50 L final solution, c = 0.40 mol/L.

Mass concentration might be expressed in g/L. Mass percent is mass of solute divided by total mass of solution, multiplied by 100%. Always read the unit carefully: mol/L, g/L, mass %, and parts per million are different ways of expressing concentration.

## Dilution

When adding solvent to a solution without losing solute, the amount of solute stays the same while volume increases and concentration falls. For ideal simple dilution:

\[
c_1V_1=c_2V_2
\]

This follows because n = cV and n remains constant. The equation uses the same concentration units and compatible volume units on both sides.

Example: to make 100 mL of 0.10 mol/L solution from a 1.0 mol/L stock, use V₁ = c₂V₂/c₁ = (0.10 × 100 mL)/1.0 = 10 mL of stock, then add solvent until the final volume is 100 mL. In lab work, use appropriate volumetric glassware and safety procedures.

## Electrolytes

An **electrolyte** forms mobile ions in solution or when molten and therefore conducts electricity. Soluble ionic compounds are often strong electrolytes because they dissociate extensively. Strong acids also ionize extensively in water. Sugar solution is a nonelectrolyte because sugar molecules do not produce significant mobile ions.

Conductivity depends on ion concentration, charge, and mobility. A solution with more ions often conducts better, but the relationship is not always directly proportional.

## Precipitation

A **precipitate** is a solid that forms from a solution. It forms when the ion concentrations exceed what the dissolved phase can maintain at equilibrium. Solubility rules are useful first approximations, while solubility-product equilibria give a deeper quantitative account.
