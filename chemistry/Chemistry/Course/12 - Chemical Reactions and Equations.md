# Chemical Reactions and Equations

A chemical reaction changes how atoms are connected and how electrons are distributed. Atoms are conserved in ordinary chemical reactions; molecules and ions can change identity.

## What changes in a reaction?

In a reaction, some existing interactions are disrupted and new interactions form. Reactant atoms are rearranged into products. The nuclei usually remain the same, so each element's atom count is conserved.

A chemical equation is a compact representation:
\[
\text{reactants} \rightarrow \text{products}
\]

The arrow means the process is represented as converting reactants into products. It does not necessarily mean a one-way reaction in all real conditions; many reactions are reversible.

## Formulas versus coefficients

A chemical formula describes the composition of one formula unit or molecule. In H₂O, the subscript 2 means two hydrogen atoms per water molecule. A coefficient in front counts how many units: 3H₂O means three water molecules, containing six H atoms and three O atoms.

Never balance an equation by changing subscripts. Changing H₂O into H₂O₂ changes water into hydrogen peroxide. Balance by changing coefficients.

## Balancing equations

Example: hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water.

Unbalanced: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Count atoms:
- left: 2 H, 2 O;
- right: 2 H, 1 O.

Put 2 before H₂O to balance oxygen: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Now the right has 4 H, so put 2 before H₂:

\[
2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O
\]

Check: 4 H and 2 O on each side. This reflects conservation of atoms.

Balancing is a counting problem, not a claim that molecules collide in those exact whole-number bundles.

## States and conditions

Equations may include state symbols:
- (s): solid;
- (l): liquid;
- (g): gas;
- (aq): dissolved in water.

Conditions such as heat, light, or a catalyst may be written above or near the arrow. A catalyst changes the reaction pathway and rate without being consumed overall.

## Common reaction patterns

- **Synthesis/combination:** multiple reactants form a product.
- **Decomposition:** one reactant forms multiple products.
- **Single displacement:** one element replaces another in a compound, when energetically and chemically possible.
- **Double displacement:** ions exchange partners; precipitation, acid-base neutralization, or gas formation may drive the process.
- **Combustion:** reaction with an oxidizer, often oxygen, frequently releasing energy.
- **Acid-base reaction:** proton transfer in the Brønsted-Lowry model.
- **Redox reaction:** electrons are transferred formally or oxidation states change.

These categories overlap. Combustion is commonly redox; an acid-base reaction may or may not be redox.

## Net ionic equations

In aqueous solution, some dissolved ions remain unchanged on both sides. These are **spectator ions**. A net ionic equation removes them to show the particles whose chemical identities actually change.

For example, when silver ions meet chloride ions, insoluble silver chloride forms:
\[
Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) \rightarrow AgCl(s)
\]

The equation says that one silver ion and one chloride ion combine into a solid formula unit in the precipitate. It does not mean only one pair exists in the whole sample.

## Evidence of reaction

Possible evidence includes gas formation, a precipitate, persistent color change, heat release or absorption, light, or a new odor. None alone proves a chemical reaction: boiling also produces bubbles, and mixing can change color physically. Interpret evidence in context and, where possible, identify the chemical species before and after.
