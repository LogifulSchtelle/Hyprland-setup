# Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Ordinary chemical reactions rearrange electrons and bonding. Nuclear chemistry studies changes in atomic nuclei, where the strong nuclear interaction, nuclear energy levels, and nuclear stability become central.

## Radioactive decay

An unstable nucleus can transform spontaneously, emitting particles or radiation. The decay is probabilistic: for a large sample, the fraction remaining follows a predictable pattern, but the exact decay time of one nucleus cannot generally be predicted.

Common decay types:
- **Alpha decay:** emission of a helium-4 nucleus; mass number falls by 4 and atomic number by 2.
- **Beta-minus decay:** a neutron transforms into a proton while emitting an electron and an antineutrino; atomic number rises by 1.
- **Beta-plus decay or electron capture:** a proton becomes a neutron through one of these processes; atomic number falls by 1.
- **Gamma emission:** an excited nucleus emits a high-energy photon; atomic and mass numbers do not change.

These processes are governed by conservation laws and nuclear energetics.

## Half-life

A radioactive isotope's **half-life** is the time required for half of the nuclei in a large sample to decay on average. After one half-life, 1/2 remains; after two, 1/4; after three, 1/8.

The decay law can be written:
\[
N=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}
\]
N₀ is the initial number, N is the expected remaining number, t is elapsed time, and t₁/₂ is half-life. This describes a statistical sample, not a guarantee that every small sample follows exact whole-number halves.

## Nuclear versus chemical energy

Nuclear changes can release much more energy per event than ordinary chemical reactions because nuclear binding-energy changes are much larger than typical chemical bond-energy changes. This does not make nuclear processes automatically safe or controllable; radiation and radioactive materials require specialized handling and regulation.

## Mass-energy equivalence

The relation E = mc² expresses that mass corresponds to energy. In a nuclear reaction, the products may have slightly less total rest mass than the reactants; the difference appears as released energy, carried by particles and radiation. Chemical mass changes are so tiny relative to ordinary masses that standard chemistry usually treats mass as conserved.

## Uses and risks

Radioisotopes are used in medical imaging, cancer treatment, tracing chemical and biological pathways, and dating materials. Risks depend on radiation type, energy, activity, exposure route, shielding, and duration. Radiation safety relies on time, distance, shielding, containment, and trained procedures; never handle unknown radioactive sources.
