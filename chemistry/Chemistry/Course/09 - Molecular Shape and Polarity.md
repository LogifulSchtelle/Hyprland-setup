# Molecular Shape and Polarity

A molecular formula tells us which atoms are present and how many, but not necessarily their three-dimensional arrangement. Shape affects polarity, intermolecular attractions, reactivity, and biological function.

## Lewis structures as bookkeeping

A Lewis structure represents valence electrons as dots and covalent bonds as lines. It helps track which atoms connect and where lone pairs sit. A **lone pair** is a pair of valence electrons not represented as a bond between two nuclei.

To build a simple Lewis structure:
1. Count the total valence electrons, adjusting for ionic charge.
2. Choose a reasonable skeleton, usually placing hydrogen at the outside.
3. Connect atoms with single bonds and subtract the electrons used.
4. Complete outer shells where appropriate.
5. Place remaining electrons on the central atom.
6. If needed, make multiple bonds and check formal charges and common exceptions.

Lewis structures are models, not photographs. Resonance means more than one Lewis drawing contributes to the actual electron distribution; the molecule does not flicker between separate drawings.

## Why molecules have shapes

Electron regions around a central atom interact. In the VSEPR model, regions of electron density arrange to reduce repulsions, giving a useful prediction of molecular geometry. This is a model, not a claim that electron clouds are hard balloons.

Common idealized electron-region geometries:
- 2 regions: linear, about 180°;
- 3 regions: trigonal planar, about 120°;
- 4 regions: tetrahedral, about 109.5°;
- 5 regions: trigonal bipyramidal;
- 6 regions: octahedral.

Lone pairs often change bond angles because their electron density is distributed differently from bonding regions.

Examples:
- CO₂ is linear. The two polar C=O bond effects point in opposite directions and cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar overall.
- H₂O is bent. Its O–H bond effects do not cancel, so water is polar.
- CH₄ is tetrahedral and symmetric, so its bond polarities cancel and it is nonpolar.
- NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal and polar.

## Bond polarity versus molecular polarity

A bond can be polar while the whole molecule is nonpolar. Molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and three-dimensional geometry. The dipole moments are vectors: they have magnitude and direction, so their contributions can cancel or reinforce.

A molecule's polarity influences how it interacts with other molecules and whether it dissolves well in a particular solvent. But “like dissolves like” is a rule of thumb, not an absolute law; size, hydrogen bonding, entropy, and other factors matter.

## Formal charge

Formal charge is a bookkeeping estimate for how valence electrons are assigned in a Lewis structure:

Formal charge = valence electrons in the free atom − nonbonding electrons − half of bonding electrons.

A good Lewis structure often minimizes unnecessary formal charges and places negative formal charge on more electronegative atoms when reasonable. Formal charge is not the same as a measured partial charge.

## Resonance and delocalization

Some molecules cannot be described adequately by one Lewis structure. In carbonate, for example, several equivalent drawings place a double bond in different positions. The actual structure has delocalized electron density and equivalent C–O bonds in the ideal ion. The drawings are accounting forms, not separate rapidly switching structures.

Delocalization can stabilize a system by spreading electron density over multiple atoms. This becomes especially important in aromatic compounds and conjugated molecules.

## Shape predicts properties

Geometry helps explain:
- whether dipoles cancel;
- which atoms are exposed for reaction;
- how molecules fit into enzymes and receptors;
- whether molecules can pack efficiently into solids;
- where intermolecular attractions are strongest.
