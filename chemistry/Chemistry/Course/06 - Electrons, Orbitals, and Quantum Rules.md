# Electrons, Orbitals, and Quantum Rules

## Why the planetary picture fails

In the old planetary picture, electrons circle the nucleus along definite paths. It is an intuitive image, but it fails to describe real atoms. A classical charged particle moving in a curved path would radiate energy, and the picture cannot explain the discrete patterns seen in atomic spectra or the structure of the periodic table.

Quantum mechanics describes electron states using a mathematical object called a **wavefunction**. The wavefunction is not directly a cloud of matter. Its squared magnitude is related to probability density, which predicts the distribution of outcomes when position is measured.

## Probability, not ignorance alone

A probability cloud is not merely a blurry image caused by a bad camera. Quantum theory does not generally assign an electron a classical path with a precise position and velocity at every instant. The state gives probabilities for possible measurements.

An **orbital** is an allowed quantum state for an electron in an atom, often represented using a probability-density picture. It is not an orbit or track. Orbitals have characteristic energies, shapes, and orientations.

The uncertainty principle states that certain pairs of quantities, such as position and momentum along the same direction, cannot both have arbitrarily small uncertainties in a quantum state. This is not just a limitation of instruments; it is part of the structure of quantum theory.

## Quantized energy

Atoms do not allow electrons to have any arbitrary bound-state energy. Only certain energy states are allowed. An electron can absorb energy and transition to a higher allowed state, or emit energy while transitioning to a lower state. The energy difference determines the photon energy:

\[
\Delta E = h\nu
\]

Here h is Planck's constant and ν (nu) is light frequency. Higher-frequency photons carry more energy. This helps explain why atoms produce characteristic emission and absorption spectra.

Energy levels are not tiny shelves physically floating around the nucleus. They are allowed states of the atom.

## Shells and subshells

The principal quantum number n labels broad energy/size groupings. Subshells are commonly named s, p, d, and f. In a simple orbital model:
- s subshell: one orbital;
- p subshell: three orbitals;
- d subshell: five orbitals;
- f subshell: seven orbitals.

Each orbital can accommodate at most two electrons with opposite spin quantum numbers. The reason involves quantum rules and the Pauli exclusion principle, not a tiny orbital's physical capacity like a seat.

The **Pauli exclusion principle** says no two electrons in the same atom can share the same complete set of four quantum numbers. The **Aufbau principle** is a useful filling guideline: electrons usually occupy lower-energy orbitals before higher-energy ones. **Hund's rule** says equal-energy orbitals tend to be singly occupied with parallel spins before pairing, in the usual ground-state filling pattern.

These rules have exceptions and complexities, especially among transition elements. Learn the logic, then learn the practical patterns.

## Why electron states have shapes

Orbital shapes arise from the mathematical solutions to the quantum problem under the relevant potential. An s orbital is spherically symmetric in its probability density. p orbitals have two main lobes separated by a nodal plane. A **node** is a region where the wavefunction is zero, so the probability density is zero there.

These shapes are not hard boundaries. Probability can extend beyond the region commonly drawn. Orbital drawings usually show a chosen contour enclosing a convenient fraction of the probability.

## Electrons are indistinguishable

Electrons are identical quantum particles. In a many-electron atom, it is often misleading to imagine each electron as a little named object assigned to a particular path. The full quantum state must respect the fact that electrons are indistinguishable fermions.

For introductory chemistry, orbital diagrams are still useful bookkeeping models. Just remember they represent allowed quantum states and occupancy, not literal boxes in space.

## From quantum rules to chemistry

The outer electrons, called **valence electrons**, are especially important in bonding and reactions. Their allowed states and energies help explain:
- why elements in the same periodic-table group often behave similarly;
- why some atoms form positive ions and others negative ions;
- why atoms form particular numbers and arrangements of bonds;
- why substances absorb and emit particular colors of light.

The periodic table is therefore not an arbitrary list. It reflects recurring patterns in electron structure.
