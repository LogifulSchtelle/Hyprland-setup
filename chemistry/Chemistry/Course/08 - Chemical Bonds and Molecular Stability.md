# Chemical Bonds and Molecular Stability

A chemical bond is not a physical string. It is a stable arrangement of atoms that arises from electromagnetic interactions and quantum rules.

## Why atoms can bond

Imagine two atoms far apart. Their interactions are small enough that each is approximately independent. As they approach, electrons are attracted to nuclei, electrons repel other electrons, nuclei repel one another, and quantum constraints affect the allowed states. The total energy changes with separation.

If some arrangement has lower energy than the separated atoms, a bond may form. The atoms do not need intentions or goals. They settle into an arrangement that can be stable under the conditions.

At extremely short distances, repulsions and quantum effects make the energy rise steeply. Therefore, a bond has a characteristic equilibrium separation rather than allowing nuclei to collapse together.

## Bond energy and the energy curve

A potential-energy curve plots the system's energy against internuclear distance. At long distance, energy approaches the separated-atom reference. As the atoms approach, attraction can lower the energy. The minimum marks an equilibrium separation. Moving the atoms apart from this minimum requires energy; pushing them much closer also requires energy.

The **bond dissociation energy** is the energy needed to separate bonded atoms in a specified process and state. Breaking a bond requires energy input. Forming a bond releases energy relative to the separated bonded atoms, when comparing the same states and conditions.

For a whole reaction, count both sides. If forming new bonds releases more energy than is needed to break old bonds, the reaction often has a negative enthalpy change. But bond energies are approximate averages, and a full reaction also includes changes in phase, interactions, and entropy.

## Covalent bonding

A **covalent bond** involves electron density shared between atoms. In H₂, the combined quantum state extends across both nuclei. Sharing does not mean electrons sit still between atoms or travel as classical balls from one atom to the other.

A **single bond** is commonly represented by one shared electron pair, a double bond by two pairs, and a triple bond by three pairs. These are useful Lewis-structure representations. The real electron distribution is quantum mechanical, and bond order is more subtle than counting drawn lines in every case.

## Ionic bonding

In an ionic compound, positive and negative ions attract electrostatically. Sodium chloride is best understood as a three-dimensional ionic lattice, not a collection of isolated NaCl molecules in the solid. Each ion interacts with many surrounding ions.

It is tempting to say “one atom gives an electron to another and that is the bond.” Electron transfer can help explain how ions arise, but the stability of the solid also depends on the collective lattice energy, the energy required to make ions, and the environment. The full energy balance determines whether the process is favorable.

## Metallic bonding

In metals, valence electrons are delocalized through the material rather than confined to one bond between two specific atoms. This mobile electron behavior helps explain electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, and metallic bonding. A simple “electron sea” model is useful, but modern descriptions use energy bands and quantum states in the solid.

## Polar covalent bonds

When two atoms share electron density unequally, the bond is polar. The more electronegative atom attracts more electron density and becomes partially negative (δ−); the other becomes partially positive (δ+). Partial charges are not usually full ionic charges.

Bond polarity depends on electronegativity difference, but the division into “covalent” and “ionic” is a continuum rather than a perfectly sharp border.

## Bonding and stability are context-dependent

A substance is not automatically stable just because one of its bonds is strong. Stability depends on the total energy of the whole system and the available reaction pathways. A thermodynamically favorable change can still be slow if an activation barrier is high.

A molecule can be metastable: it persists because a pathway to a lower-energy arrangement is kinetically difficult. This is why many fuels and oxidizers can coexist until a suitable reaction pathway is initiated.
