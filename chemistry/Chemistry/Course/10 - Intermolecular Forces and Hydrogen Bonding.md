# Intermolecular Forces and Hydrogen Bonding

A molecule's internal bonds and the attractions between separate molecules are different things. This distinction explains boiling points, viscosity, surface tension, and many solubility patterns.

## Intramolecular versus intermolecular

**Intramolecular** means within a molecule: covalent bonds connect atoms in water. **Intermolecular** means between molecules: neighboring water molecules attract each other.

Boiling liquid water does not normally require breaking every O–H covalent bond. It requires separating molecules from one another enough to enter the gas phase. Chemical reactions, by contrast, change chemical connectivity and may break or form covalent bonds.

## London dispersion forces

Electrons are distributed quantum mechanically, and their instantaneous distribution can fluctuate. A temporary uneven distribution can induce a correlated distribution in a nearby particle, producing attraction. These are **London dispersion forces**.

Dispersion forces occur between all atoms and molecules, even nonpolar ones. They tend to become stronger for larger, more polarizable electron clouds, though shape and packing also matter. This helps explain why larger nonpolar molecules often have higher boiling points than smaller ones in related series.

## Dipole-dipole attractions

A polar molecule has a permanent dipole: one region is partially positive and another partially negative. Neighboring polar molecules can orient so opposite partial charges attract. Their motion and thermal energy prevent the molecules from remaining locked in one perfect orientation in a liquid.

## Hydrogen bonding

A **hydrogen bond** is a particularly important directional attraction that commonly occurs when hydrogen covalently bonded to a strongly electronegative atom such as N, O, or F interacts with a lone pair on another suitable atom.

In water, oxygen pulls shared electron density away from hydrogen, creating partial charges. The hydrogen of one molecule can interact attractively with an oxygen lone-pair region on another. This network contributes to water's unusual boiling point, surface tension, heat capacity, and ice structure.

Hydrogen bonds are not the same as the O–H covalent bonds inside a water molecule. They are generally weaker than covalent bonds, but many hydrogen bonds together can have major effects.

## Ion-dipole interactions

An ion can attract the partial charges of a polar molecule. When table salt dissolves in water, water molecules orient around Na⁺ and Cl⁻, forming hydration shells. These ion-dipole interactions help stabilize separated ions in solution.

Dissolving occurs when the overall balance of energy and entropy favors the solution relative to the original solid and liquid. A strong interaction alone does not guarantee unlimited solubility.

## How forces affect properties

Stronger effective attractions between particles often mean more energy is needed to separate them, tending to increase boiling point and reduce volatility. They can also influence viscosity and surface tension.

But comparisons require care: molecular size, shape, polarizability, and structure all matter. A single slogan such as “hydrogen bonds always dominate” is too simple.

## Why oil and water separate

Water is polar and forms a network of favorable interactions. Many oils are largely nonpolar and interact with water less favorably. Mixing them disrupts some water-water interactions and does not replace them with enough favorable oil-water interactions to compensate; entropy also contributes to the final outcome. The mixture separates into phases.

The phrase “like dissolves like” summarizes a pattern: substances with compatible interaction types often mix well. It is a guide, not a fundamental law.
