# What Does It Mean to Share Electrons?

This lesson follows [[Electron Cloud]] and leads into [[Chemical Bonds]]. The main question is: when we say two atoms **share electrons**, what is actually being shared?

## 1. Start with the word “cloud”

An **electron cloud** is a picture of the probability of detecting an electron in different places. It is not a mist made of tiny particles, and it does not show a little electron's exact path.

**Probability** means how likely an outcome is. If a region has a higher probability density, an electron is more likely to be detected there when a measurement is made.

A **probability density** is the probability distribution described per unit of space. For now, remember the simple version: it tells us where an electron is more or less likely to be detected.

## 2. What changes when two atoms approach?

Imagine two hydrogen atoms far apart. Each has one proton in its nucleus and one electron described by a quantum state around that nucleus.

When the atoms are brought closer, each electron is affected by **both** positively charged nuclei. The electrons also interact with one another, and the nuclei repel one another because both have positive charge.

The electrons are no longer best described as belonging to two completely independent atoms. We must describe the combined system.

## 3. What does “shared” mean?

In a hydrogen molecule (H₂), the allowed electron arrangement can extend across both nuclei. The electrons are part of the molecule's combined quantum state, rather than being assigned to one atom as if they were possessions.

This is what we mean by **shared electron density**: the distribution associated with the electrons extends across the bonded atoms. It does **not** mean:
- the electrons sit motionless like beads between the nuclei;
- each electron takes turns travelling from one atom to the other;
- there is a tiny physical bridge connecting the atoms;
- we can give each electron a simple classical orbit and track it along that orbit.

The quantum description predicts the probabilities of measurement outcomes. It is not a blurry photograph of a tiny planet.

## 4. Why can sharing help make a bond?

The combined system has several contributions to its energy:
- attraction between each electron and the positively charged nuclei;
- repulsion between the two electrons;
- repulsion between the two nuclei;
- quantum effects that restrict the allowed electron states.

At some separations, the combined arrangement has **lower energy** than the separated atoms. For hydrogen, this produces a stable range of separation: the hydrogen molecule.

If the nuclei are pushed much too close, the energy rises sharply. The atoms do not simply collapse into one another.

The key idea is not that “sharing is always good.” It is that a particular quantum arrangement, together with all the attractions and repulsions, can make the whole system more stable.

## 5. A picture that helps, and where it fails

Think of two people holding a large blanket between them. The blanket can span both people rather than belonging to just one. This may help you picture something extending across two centers.

But the analogy has limits: electrons are not blankets, and an electron cloud is not a material sheet. The real description is mathematical and quantum-mechanical. Use the analogy only to remember the idea of **one arrangement extending across both atoms**.

## 6. A small but important distinction

**Electron cloud** is a broad picture of where an electron may be detected. **Electron density** describes the spatial distribution used when discussing a system's electrons. **Orbital** means an allowed quantum state, not a path around the nucleus.

In a molecule, scientists use molecular orbitals and other quantum tools to describe electron arrangements. We do not need the equations yet to understand the basic concept.

## 7. Put it in your own words

Try answering before opening another lesson:

1. When scientists say that electrons are “shared,” what exactly does that mean?
2. Name two things “shared” does **not** mean.
3. Why must we include electron-nucleus attraction, electron-electron repulsion, and nucleus-nucleus repulsion?
4. Why is the blanket analogy useful, and where does it stop being accurate?
5. Is the electron cloud a physical fog or a representation of probabilities?

## Short version

In a covalent bond, electrons are described by a quantum arrangement that can extend across multiple nuclei. This arrangement, together with electric attractions and repulsions, can give the combined system a lower-energy stable structure. **Sharing describes the quantum arrangement, not little balls sitting still between atoms.**
