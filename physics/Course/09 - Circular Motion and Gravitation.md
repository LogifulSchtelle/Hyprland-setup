# 09 — Circular Motion and Gravitation

## Turning requires acceleration

Velocity includes direction. An object travelling in a circle at constant speed continuously changes velocity, so it accelerates toward the circle's centre. For speed (v) and radius (r), centripetal acceleration is (a_c=v^2/r).

The required inward net force is (F_c=mv^2/r). “Centripetal force” is not a new kind of force; it is the name for the inward net force supplied by gravity, tension, friction, or a combination.

## Newtonian gravity

Every pair of masses attracts:
(F=G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2}),
where (G) is the universal gravitational constant and (r) is the separation between their centres for spherical bodies or point-mass approximations.

The inverse-square relationship means doubling separation reduces force to one quarter. Doubling one mass doubles the force.

## Orbits

An orbiting object is continually falling toward the body it circles, while its sideways velocity causes it to keep missing the surface. For a circular orbit around mass (M), setting gravity equal to the needed centripetal force gives (v=\sqrt{GM/r}).

Astronauts in orbit experience apparent weightlessness because they and their spacecraft are in continuous free fall together, not because gravity has vanished.

## Tides and limits

Tides arise mainly from differences in gravitational pull across Earth, not simply from the Moon pulling water upward at one location. At extreme gravity or high precision, Newtonian gravity is replaced by general relativity.
