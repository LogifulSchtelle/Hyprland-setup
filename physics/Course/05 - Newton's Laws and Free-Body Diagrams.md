# 05 — Newton's Laws and Free-Body Diagrams

## First law: inertia

An object maintains constant velocity unless a net external force acts on it. Constant velocity includes staying still. Motion does not require a continuing force; changing motion does. Friction often hides this fact in everyday life.

## Second law: how interactions change motion

For constant mass, (\sum\vec F=m\vec a). The net force is the vector sum of all external forces on the chosen object. Forces in opposite directions can cancel. If the net force is zero, acceleration is zero, not necessarily velocity.

One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s².

## Third law: interactions come in pairs

If object A pushes on object B, B pushes back on A with equal magnitude and opposite direction. The pair acts on **different objects**, so the forces do not cancel each other on one free-body diagram.

## Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram shows the chosen object isolated, with arrows for forces exerted on it. Include actual interactions, not velocity arrows or forces the object exerts on other things.

Example: a book resting on a table has Earth's gravitational force downward and the table's normal force upward. They balance if the book has no vertical acceleration. The normal force is not always equal to weight; it depends on the situation.

## Worked example

A 4 kg cart experiences a net horizontal force of 12 N. Its acceleration is (a=F/m=12/4=3\,\text{m/s}^2) in the direction of the net force.

## Common traps

- A force is an interaction, not a stored property of an object.
- Action–reaction forces act on different objects.
- Motion itself is not a force.
- Balanced forces mean zero acceleration, not necessarily rest.

## Checkpoint

Draw a free-body diagram for a lamp hanging motionless from a cord. Identify the two interactions and explain why the lamp is not accelerating.
