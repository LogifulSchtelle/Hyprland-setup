# 02 — Measurement, Units, and Dimensions

## Quantities need both numbers and units

Saying a table is 2 long is incomplete. Two metres and two centimetres differ by a factor of 100. A measurement is a number multiplied by a unit.

The SI system uses seven base units: metre (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, ampere (A) for electric current, kelvin (K) for thermodynamic temperature, mole (mol) for amount of substance, and candela (cd) for luminous intensity.

Many units are derived:
- Speed: m/s
- Acceleration: m/s²
- Force: newton, N = kg·m/s²
- Energy: joule, J = kg·m²/s²
- Power: watt, W = J/s
- Pressure: pascal, Pa = N/m²
- Charge: coulomb, C = A·s
- Voltage: volt, V = J/C

## Dimensional analysis

Dimensions describe the kind of quantity, independently of the unit system. Distance has dimension L, time T, mass M. Speed has dimension L/T. Any valid addition must combine like dimensions; both sides of an equation must have matching dimensions.

For example, (d=vt) is dimensionally sensible because (length/time) × time = length. Dimensional agreement does not prove an equation is correct, but disagreement proves it is wrong.

## Conversions

Convert units by multiplying by a ratio equal to one:
(72\,\text{km/h} = 72\times\frac{1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} = 20\,\text{m/s}).

Keep units in intermediate steps. They act like algebraic labels and expose many mistakes.

## Significant figures and uncertainty

Do not report more precision than the measurement supports. If a length is measured as about 2.3 m, a calculated result with eight decimal places is not automatically more trustworthy.
