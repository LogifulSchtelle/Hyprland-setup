# 20 — Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity

## Inside the nucleus
Nuclei contain protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons. Protons repel electrically, but the strong nuclear interaction binds nucleons at very short distances. Stability depends on the balance of these interactions and the nucleus's structure.

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with different neutron numbers. They share proton number and broadly similar chemistry, but can have different masses and nuclear stability.

## Radioactive decay
Unstable nuclei transform spontaneously. Common forms include:
- **Alpha decay:** emission of a helium-4 nucleus.
- **Beta-minus decay:** a neutron transforms into a proton while an electron and antineutrino are emitted.
- **Gamma emission:** an excited nucleus releases energy as a high-energy photon.

An individual decay is probabilistic, but large populations follow predictable statistical patterns.

## Half-life
Half-life is the time for half the undecayed nuclei in a large sample to decay on average. After \(n\) half-lives, the remaining fraction is \((1/2)^n\). Half-life is characteristic of the nuclear state and, under ordinary conditions, does not depend on the starting amount.

## Fission and fusion
Fission splits a heavy nucleus; fusion combines light nuclei. Both can release energy when the products have greater total binding energy per nucleon. Nuclear reactors, stars and medical imaging use nuclear physics in very different ways.

Ionizing radiation can damage tissue, but risk depends on radiation type, energy, dose, route and exposure duration. It also has carefully controlled medical, industrial and research uses. Never handle unknown radioactive materials or attempt improvised radiation experiments.
