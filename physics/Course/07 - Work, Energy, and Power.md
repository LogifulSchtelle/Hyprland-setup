# 07 — Work, Energy, and Power

## Work is energy transferred by a force

For a constant force and straight displacement, (W=Fd\cos\theta), where \(\theta\) is the angle between force and displacement. Force in the direction of motion does positive work; opposite force does negative work; perpendicular force does zero work.

Work is measured in joules: 1 J = 1 N·m. Carrying a bag horizontally at constant height may feel tiring, but the upward force you exert does zero mechanical work on the bag in the idealized horizontal-displacement model. Your body still uses chemical energy internally.

## Kinetic and potential energy

Kinetic energy of an object moving at speed (v): (K=\frac12mv^2).

Near Earth's surface, gravitational potential-energy change is (\Delta U_g=mg\Delta h). Elastic potential energy in an ideal spring is (U_s=\frac12kx^2).

Potential energy belongs to a system and depends on a chosen reference level. Only differences in gravitational potential energy matter in ordinary mechanics.

## Work–energy theorem

Net work on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: (W_{\text{net}}=\Delta K). This is a powerful alternative to solving every force and acceleration in detail.

When only conservative forces transfer energy within the system, kinetic plus potential energy remains constant. With friction, mechanical energy can become thermal energy; total energy is not destroyed.

## Power

Power is the rate of energy transfer: (P=\Delta E/\Delta t). One watt is one joule per second. Two machines may do the same work, but the one that does it faster delivers greater average power.

## Example

A 2 kg object rises 3 m. Its gravitational potential energy increases by about (mgh=2(9.81)(3)=58.9\) J. Ignoring losses, at least that much energy must be transferred to it.
