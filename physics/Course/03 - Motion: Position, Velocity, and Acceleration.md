# 03 — Motion: Position, Velocity, and Acceleration

## Position is relative to a reference

To describe motion, choose a reference frame: an origin and directions for measuring positions. A passenger sitting on a moving bus is at rest relative to the bus but moving relative to the road. Neither description is automatically wrong; the reference frame differs.

**Distance** is the total path length. **Displacement** is the change from initial to final position and includes direction. Walk 3 m east and then 3 m west: distance is 6 m, displacement is zero.

## Speed and velocity

Average speed = total distance / elapsed time.

Average velocity = displacement / elapsed time.

Velocity is a vector: it includes direction. Instantaneous velocity describes motion at a particular moment. A speedometer gives speed, not the full velocity vector.

## Acceleration

Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes:
(a_{\text{avg}}=\Delta v/\Delta t).

A change in velocity can mean a change in speed, direction, or both. A car moving around a bend at constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes.

For constant acceleration:
- (v=v_0+at)
- (\Delta x=v_0t+\frac12at^2)
- (v^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x)

These equations apply only when acceleration is constant. Symbols: (v_0) initial velocity, (v) final velocity, (a) acceleration, (t) elapsed time, and (\Delta x) displacement.

## Worked example

A cyclist starts at 2 m/s and accelerates at 1.5 m/s² for 4 s. Final velocity is (2+(1.5)(4)=8\) m/s. Displacement is (2(4)+\frac12(1.5)(4^2)=20\) m.

## Common traps

- Negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down. Compare acceleration's sign with velocity's sign.
- Zero displacement does not imply zero distance travelled.
- A large speed does not necessarily mean a large acceleration.
