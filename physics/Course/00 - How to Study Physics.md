# 00 — How to Study Physics

## The purpose

Physics becomes confusing when equations arrive before the ideas they describe. Instead, build a chain: **observation → question → model → measurable quantities → relationship → prediction → test**.

A model deliberately leaves details out. Treating a ball as a point object is useful when its size and rotation do not matter. It is not a claim that the ball has no size.

## A reliable problem-solving routine

1. **Read for the situation.** What is happening physically?
2. **Choose the system.** Which object or collection are we studying?
3. **Sketch it.** Mark directions, contacts, paths, and known values.
4. **Name the quantities.** Include units and directions where needed.
5. **Choose a principle.** Newton's laws? Energy conservation? A circuit law?
6. **Write the relationship symbolically first.**
7. **Substitute values and calculate.**
8. **Check units, sign, scale, and limiting cases.**
9. **Interpret the result in ordinary language.**

## Why units matter

Units are part of the meaning. A speed of 5 metres per second is not the same kind of quantity as 5 metres. If an equation says a distance equals a time plus a mass, something is wrong before any arithmetic happens.

## Memorization versus understanding

Memorize standard definitions and a few foundational relationships, but derive and interpret as much as possible. Ask what would happen if a quantity doubled, vanished, or reversed direction.

## Checkpoint

- Why is a sketch useful even when the problem contains all its data?
- What is the difference between a model and reality?
- Why can a numerically correct calculation still be physically wrong?
