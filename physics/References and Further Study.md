# Physics References and Further Study

These notes are an introductory learning path written to explain the ideas in plain language. For verification, deeper derivations and alternative explanations, use established educational and scientific references.

## Free learning resources
- [OpenStax University Physics](https://openstax.org/subjects/science) — free, structured textbooks with worked examples; useful for algebra-based and calculus-based study.
- [The Feynman Lectures on Physics](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/) — a famous conceptual and mathematical treatment from Caltech. Some chapters are demanding; use them as a second pass rather than a barrier to starting.
- [HyperPhysics](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/) — concise concept maps and topic summaries; good for connecting ideas, but cross-check details when needed.
- [PhET Interactive Simulations](https://phet.colorado.edu/) — interactive simulations for mechanics, circuits, waves, gases and more. Use them to test predictions, not merely to watch animations.
- [NIST Fundamental Physical Constants](https://physics.nist.gov/cuu/Constants/) — authoritative values for physical constants.
- [CERN: Physics](https://home.cern/science/physics) — accessible introductions to particle physics and current research.
- [NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/) — astronomy, planetary science, cosmology and space missions.

## How to use a reference
1. First write down your current explanation or prediction.
2. Read the reference and identify what it confirms or changes.
3. Check whether the source assumes algebra, calculus or prior physics.
4. Work one problem without looking at the solution.
5. Keep a short note of the misconception you corrected.

## A note about models
Introductory equations are often idealizations. For example, constant-acceleration equations assume acceleration stays constant; Ohm's law applies directly only to ohmic components under suitable conditions; Bernoulli's equation has assumptions about fluid flow. A formula can be correct and still be wrong for a particular situation if its assumptions do not hold.
